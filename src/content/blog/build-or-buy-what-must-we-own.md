For decades, technology leaders have relied on a relatively simple question when deciding how to introduce a new capability into an organization: should we build it ourselves, or should we buy it? It was a useful question because building software was expensive. A meaningful enterprise application required engineers, architects, infrastructure, security, testing, deployment and years of maintenance. The economics naturally forced organizations to be selective about what they chose to own.

AI is beginning to change those economics. We can now create applications, workflows and agents at a speed that would have seemed unrealistic only a few years ago. Small teams can prototype working solutions in days. AI coding tools can generate interfaces, APIs, database structures and tests. Increasingly agentic development environments can take a description of a business problem and produce significant portions of a functioning solution.

This is an extraordinary shift, but it also introduces a new problem. As building becomes easier, organizations are going to want to build more. The barrier that once forced us to ask whether something was really worth developing is disappearing. Soon, "we can build it" will no longer be a meaningful argument because the answer will increasingly be yes.

That is why I think the traditional Build versus Buy question needs to change. The more important question for the AI era is not whether we can build something. It is what must we own?

## The Cost of Creation Is Not the Cost of Ownership

One of the easiest mistakes to make in this new environment will be confusing the falling cost of creating software with the cost of owning it. AI is dramatically reducing the first. I am not convinced it reduces the second nearly as much.

An internal application that once required six months of development may soon take six days to create. That is transformative. It allows experimentation to happen closer to the business problem and gives people who understand the work the ability to participate directly in designing solutions. It also changes the economics of solving smaller operational problems that previously would never have justified a traditional engineering investment.

But once that application becomes part of an important business process, a different cost begins to accumulate. Someone has to secure it, monitor it and support it. Someone has to understand what happens when an API changes or when the underlying model is updated. Access to data has to be governed. Integrations need to be maintained. Users need support. Regulatory requirements evolve. Business processes change. What began as a remarkably inexpensive prototype slowly becomes another piece of enterprise infrastructure.

The application may have taken a weekend to create. The organization may own the consequences of that decision for the next decade.

This is where I believe AI First organizations will need considerably more discipline than the technology itself appears to require. The ability to build almost anything cannot become an excuse to own everything. If organizations do not develop that discipline, AI could create an interesting contradiction: the greatest increase in software development productivity we have ever experienced could also produce the greatest accumulation of technical debt.

## What Is Worth Owning?

The answer is clearly not to stop building. In fact, I believe organizations should build more in the AI era. But what they build should increasingly be determined by what they want to accumulate.

Some capabilities become more valuable every time they are used because they capture something about how the organization works. They capture decisions, exceptions, patterns, customer behaviours and the subtle judgment experienced employees use when solving difficult problems. Over time, those capabilities become more than software. They become repositories of organizational intelligence.

Healthcare implementation provides a useful example. Imagine an organization that has completed thousands of complex technology implementations. Over time, its people have learned which configuration choices work best in different environments, which customer decisions tend to create downstream problems, where integrations typically fail and which questions experienced consultants ask before anyone else realizes those questions matter.

Most of that knowledge does not exist neatly inside a database. Some of it lives in documentation. Some lives in project histories and customer conversations. A significant amount simply lives inside the heads of experienced employees. It is the accumulated result of solving similar but never identical problems thousands of times.

That knowledge is enormously valuable, and this is where I think the definition of what we should "build" begins to change. The strategic asset may not be the application itself. The application could eventually be replaced. The interface could change. The underlying model almost certainly will change. What matters is the organizational intelligence the system is continuously capturing and refining.

That is what I mean by building what compounds. We should think seriously about owning capabilities where every interaction makes the organization smarter and where surrendering that capability would also mean surrendering some portion of the organization's accumulated learning.

## Not Everything Needs to Become Intellectual Property

There is an equally important discipline on the other side of this argument. Organizations perform hundreds of functions that matter enormously but provide little meaningful differentiation. Payroll matters. Expense management matters. Scheduling matters. Identity management matters. CRM matters. The fact that a capability is important does not automatically mean owning it creates strategic advantage.

AI is going to blur this distinction because rebuilding common enterprise capabilities will become deceptively easy. A team may look at an existing application and conclude that it could create something more tailored in a few weeks. In many cases, that will probably be true. But that still does not answer whether the organization should own it.

The better question is what the organization gains from ownership. If owning the capability does not accumulate differentiated knowledge, strengthen a unique workflow or create meaningful strategic advantage, then building it may simply convert a product subscription into a permanent maintenance obligation.

This is why I do not believe being AI First means trying to build everything with AI. Sometimes the most mature AI decision will be deliberately choosing not to build something, even when doing so has become surprisingly easy. The scarcity is shifting away from development capacity and toward organizational attention. Leaders will have to decide where that attention produces the greatest return.

## Healthcare Adds Another Dimension

There is also a category where ownership cannot be considered only through the lens of differentiation or cost. In healthcare, some workflows carry consequences that fundamentally change the decision.

Consider AI involved in medication workflows, clinical decision support, resident transitions, regulatory reporting or the movement of sensitive health information. Demonstrating that an agent can successfully perform the task is only the beginning. The more important questions appear once that capability enters a real operating environment.

What happens when the agent is wrong? How is the output validated? How can the decision be audited six months later? What information was available to the system when the decision was made? What happens when the underlying model changes? How does the organization identify behaviour that falls outside expected boundaries?

These questions move quickly beyond software engineering. They involve clinical knowledge, regulation, security, governance, risk and operational experience. There will be situations where an organization is technically capable of building a solution but where carrying the entire responsibility for that solution makes little sense.

This is where partnership becomes strategically important. A strong partner is not simply providing technology. They may be contributing specialized expertise, regulatory knowledge, clinical understanding or operating experience that would take years for the organization to recreate. In those circumstances, sharing some control may actually create a stronger system than maximizing ownership.

As AI moves from generating information to taking action, I believe this distinction will matter much more. The question will increasingly shift from "Can the AI do this?" to "Who should be responsible for the system that allows the AI to do this?"

## There Is Also Value in Not Deciding Yet

The speed of AI introduces another option that technology leaders are often uncomfortable with: waiting.

We tend to associate technology leadership with movement. We build, launch, experiment and transform. Waiting can feel like falling behind, particularly when every week seems to bring another major advance in models, agents or enterprise platforms.

But rapid technological change creates risk in both directions. Organizations can move too slowly, but they can also commit too early. Capabilities that required substantial custom engineering eighteen months ago are increasingly appearing as standard features inside platforms. Infrastructure that once looked like a strategic investment can quickly become something available through configuration.

There will therefore be areas where waiting is entirely rational. The important distinction is between waiting and standing still. Strategic waiting means continuing to experiment, preparing the data, documenting workflows, developing people and understanding how the technology is evolving. The organization is building readiness without prematurely locking itself into an architecture that may become obsolete before it produces meaningful value.

Standing still is different. Standing still means the technology develops while the organization does not. One approach preserves optionality. The other creates a capability gap. Leaders will need to become comfortable distinguishing between the two.

## The Real Decision Is About Risk

Once we look at Build, Buy, Partner and Wait this way, they stop looking like procurement choices and start looking like different forms of risk allocation.

Building gives the organization greater control, but it also creates ownership and technical debt. Buying can accelerate adoption and reduce development effort, but it creates dependency on another organization's roadmap and architecture. Partnering can bring expertise and distribute execution risk, but it means accepting shared control. Waiting preserves optionality, but it introduces the possibility that competitors or the market develop capabilities faster than you do.

There is no option without a tradeoff, and I think that is an important realization for AI strategy. Leadership is not about finding the architecture with no risk. It is about deciding which risks the organization is prepared to carry in exchange for the capabilities it believes matter most.

That brings the conversation back to ownership because the most consequential decision is not necessarily which technology we choose. It is deciding which parts of our organizational intelligence we are willing to place outside our control.

## The Model Is Not the Moat

There was a period when enterprise AI strategy focused heavily on access to models. Organizations debated which model was best, which platform they should standardize on and whether access to a more capable model might create a meaningful competitive advantage.

I think that advantage will continue to shrink.

Most organizations will eventually have access to extremely capable models. They will have sophisticated agents and enterprise platforms capable of orchestrating complex work. Models will continue to improve, but those improvements will also become broadly available. If two competing organizations can access similar intelligence, then access to intelligence itself becomes a weak source of differentiation.

The more interesting source of advantage is context.

Context is the knowledge your organization has accumulated about how work actually happens. It is the relationship between your data, workflows, customers, decisions and history. It includes the exceptions experienced employees recognize immediately and the reasons certain processes exist even when those reasons were never formally documented.

Two organizations may use the same model and even the same enterprise AI platform. But if one organization has captured and structured its institutional knowledge while the other has not, those two AI systems will behave very differently.

The model provides intelligence. Context gives that intelligence meaning.

This is why I increasingly believe that organizations should worry less about owning models and much more about owning the context that makes those models useful.

## From Systems of Record to Systems of Context

Enterprise architecture has historically been organized around systems of record. We know where the customer record lives, where the employee record lives, where the clinical record lives and where the financial record lives. Entire technology strategies have been designed around protecting and connecting those sources of truth.

AI introduces another architectural question that may eventually become just as important: where does organizational context live?

Where do we capture not simply the decision, but the reasoning behind the decision? Where do we preserve the exceptions that experienced employees understand? Where do workflows describe how work actually happens rather than how the process diagram says it should happen? Where does the knowledge accumulated across thousands of customer interactions become reusable organizational intelligence?

This is one of the reasons I continue to believe Knowledge Protocol Engineering will become increasingly important. Organizations need mechanisms for turning human knowledge, operating rules, decisions, workflows and experience into structured context that AI systems can understand and use. Without that layer, organizations may deploy increasingly intelligent models while continuously asking those models to operate without the institutional knowledge required to make that intelligence relevant.

The models will change. The agents will change. The platforms will change. Some of the applications we are building today will disappear entirely.

The organizational intelligence should remain.

## What Must We Own?

This is why I think the traditional Build versus Buy conversation is evolving into something much more strategic. Build, Buy, Partner and Wait are useful choices, but none of them should be the starting point.

The starting point should be understanding what the organization cannot afford to lose.

Which knowledge differentiates us? Which workflows continuously make us smarter? Which data relationships would be difficult for a competitor to recreate? Which parts of our institutional knowledge become more valuable as AI becomes more capable? And which capabilities are simply infrastructure that someone else can operate better than we can?

The answers will also change. Something worth building today may become a commodity tomorrow. Something we buy today may eventually become strategically important enough to require greater control. Something we partner on may mature enough to operate internally. Something we deliberately wait on may reach the point where waiting becomes more dangerous than acting.

AI strategy therefore cannot become a one time architecture decision. It has to become an ongoing discipline of deciding where ownership creates value and where ownership simply creates obligation.

As AI makes building easier, the temptation will be to measure progress by how much we create.

I think the more mature organizations will measure something different.

They will know what to build, what to buy, where to partner and when to wait because they will have answered the harder question first.

What is too important not to own?
