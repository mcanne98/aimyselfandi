*Why the next leadership challenge is not AI adoption, but deciding what becomes possible when work changes.*

For the past few years, organizations have invested considerable effort in making artificial intelligence part of everyday work. We have encouraged employees to experiment with new tools, identified opportunities to automate repetitive activities, developed AI strategies, and introduced intelligent systems into business processes. Much of the conversation has focused on adoption, productivity, and demonstrating that AI can deliver measurable value.

And increasingly, it is working.

Tasks that once required hours can now be completed in minutes. Documents are generated faster, information is analyzed more efficiently, and activities that previously depended on manual coordination are beginning to be executed by intelligent agents. Across industries, organizations are discovering that AI can significantly change the effort required to complete familiar work.

But I believe we are approaching a leadership challenge that deserves considerably more attention.

**What happens to the work when AI successfully takes over a meaningful portion of it?**

We tend to assume that improving productivity automatically improves the organization. If employees can accomplish more in less time, surely the business becomes more efficient. If intelligent systems can execute activities previously performed by people, surely the organization becomes more capable.

Yet the relationship between individual productivity and organizational performance is not nearly that simple.

An organization can become remarkably efficient at completing individual tasks while retaining the same processes, dependencies, approval structures, and operating constraints that existed before AI was introduced. Employees may spend less time preparing information, only to discover that the next stage of the process remains unchanged. Teams may produce significantly more output without necessarily delivering better outcomes.

The technology has improved how the work gets done, but the organization has not reconsidered how work should happen.

This is why I believe the next phase of AI transformation will be less about deploying technology and more about understanding how successful automation changes the responsibilities of people, the structure of processes, and the allocation of organizational capacity.

**We have spent considerable effort preparing AI to do the work. We have spent far less effort preparing organizations for what happens when AI no longer needs us to do all of it.**

## The Capacity Paradox

Consider a team responsible for delivering customer implementations. A significant portion of its work involves gathering requirements, reviewing documentation, preparing configurations, validating information, and coordinating activities across different functions.

Now imagine that the organization successfully introduces AI into these activities. Requirements can be interpreted automatically, configuration instructions can be prepared in minutes, and data validation can identify discrepancies before they reach the implementation team.

The productivity improvement is substantial. Activities that previously consumed several hours can now be completed with significantly less manual effort.

From a technology perspective, the initiative has succeeded. But from an organizational perspective, a new set of questions emerges.

What happens to the time that has been released? Does the team take on more implementations? Do employees spend more time solving complex customer problems? Does the organization reduce delivery timelines? Are the original approval processes still necessary? Should the responsibilities of implementation specialists and project managers evolve?

If these questions remain unanswered, the organization may simply continue operating in the same way, with employees completing individual activities faster while waiting for the same approvals, handoffs, and decisions.

This is what I would describe as **the Capacity Paradox**.

Artificial intelligence can create additional capacity without necessarily creating additional organizational value.

The distinction matters because time saved and value realized are not the same thing. When an employee saves several hours through automation, that time does not automatically become financial savings, better customer experiences, or increased business capacity. It represents an opportunity that still requires a decision.

An organization may choose to reinvest that capacity in growth, quality, customer relationships, or innovation. It may also choose to reduce operating costs or create more sustainable workloads. Each represents a different outcome, and each requires a deliberate approach to how work is organized.

Without that direction, additional capacity can simply become additional activity.

Employees may be expected to produce more documentation, attend more meetings, complete more analyses, or manage more requests because technology has made those activities easier. The volume of work increases, but the underlying organizational complexity remains.

The danger is that we begin confusing the ability to do more work with the ability to deliver more value.

**AI creates the possibility of capacity. Leadership determines what that capacity becomes.**

## When Productivity Moves the Bottleneck

One of the most important lessons from process improvement is that making one activity faster does not necessarily improve the performance of the entire process.

Every organization operates through a collection of interconnected activities. Information moves between teams, decisions require approval, dependencies must be resolved, and outcomes depend on multiple people and systems working together.

When AI improves one part of this structure, the constraints often move somewhere else.

Consider a customer support organization that introduces AI to help agents investigate technical issues. The AI retrieves relevant information, analyzes previous incidents, recommends corrective actions, and prepares responses. As a result, the support team can investigate significantly more cases in the same amount of time.

But what happens when those investigations identify product defects requiring engineering intervention?

The engineering team may now receive more detailed and better documented escalations, but its capacity to resolve them has not necessarily changed. The support function becomes more productive while the engineering queue continues growing.

The organization has accelerated the discovery of problems without necessarily improving its ability to resolve them.

A similar situation can occur in healthcare.

Imagine an AI documentation assistant that reduces the time clinicians spend preparing encounter notes. The immediate benefit may be meaningful. But whether this translates into additional patient access, improved clinical interactions, reduced administrative burden, or more sustainable workloads depends on how the surrounding care process is managed.

Increasing appointment capacity, for example, may place additional demand on scheduling, referrals, laboratory services, or other clinical resources. The productivity improvement in one activity does not eliminate the dependencies that determine the broader patient experience.

This is where leadership must begin looking beyond individual AI use cases.

The question should not simply be how much faster a task can be completed, but whether the entire process has become more effective.

If we automate an activity while preserving every existing handoff, approval, and dependency, we may have improved the speed of execution without improving the design of the work.

And in some cases, the increased volume created by automation can expose weaknesses that were previously less visible.

This is not an argument against automation. It is an argument for understanding its organizational consequences.

The real opportunity appears when leaders use productivity improvements as a reason to reconsider the process itself. Which steps are still necessary? Which decisions can be made earlier? Where does human judgment create value? Which responsibilities should remain within the team, and which should move to intelligent systems?

**The next productivity breakthrough may not come from making AI more capable. It may come from removing the organizational constraints that prevent its capabilities from translating into outcomes.**

## The Work Changes Before the Organization Does

There is another dimension to successful AI implementation that I believe leaders need to consider more deliberately.

When technology begins performing activities that previously defined someone's role, the nature of that role changes, whether the organization acknowledges it or not.

Consider a technical professional whose responsibilities have historically involved preparing configurations, reviewing integration requirements, documenting decisions, and validating implementation data.

Over time, AI may become capable of performing much of the initial preparation and analysis. The professional is no longer required to spend the same amount of time producing these outputs.

But the need for expertise does not disappear.

Someone still needs to understand the customer's environment, evaluate architectural implications, identify risks, validate exceptions, and determine whether the proposed solution will deliver the intended outcome.

The role gradually shifts from producing the work toward directing, evaluating, and improving how the work is performed.

This distinction is important because organizations often introduce AI without revisiting the responsibilities, performance expectations, or career development pathways associated with the roles being affected.

Employees are encouraged to become more productive, yet they may continue being evaluated using measures established when manual execution represented the majority of their contribution.

A project manager may no longer need to spend hours preparing status reports, but could become significantly more valuable by identifying delivery risks earlier and improving coordination across teams. A customer support professional may spend less time retrieving knowledge and more time resolving complex problems, improving customer relationships, and identifying recurring product issues.

In both cases, the nature of the contribution evolves.

The challenge for leaders is recognizing that these changes require more than AI training.

Employees need clarity about which activities are becoming less important, which responsibilities are emerging, and how their experience can be applied in new ways. Managers need to reconsider how performance is evaluated and how talent is developed.

We also need to be careful not to assume that every employee will naturally transition into more complex responsibilities simply because routine work becomes automated.

Capabilities such as architectural thinking, complex problem solving, process design, and judgment develop through experience, practice, and deliberate learning. If AI takes over many of the foundational activities through which people traditionally acquired that experience, organizations will need to reconsider how future expertise is developed.

A junior professional who previously learned by reviewing hundreds of configuration files may no longer perform that work manually. But if we eliminate the learning experience without replacing it, we risk creating professionals who can supervise an AI generated outcome without fully understanding the reasoning required to evaluate it.

This creates a responsibility to redesign learning alongside work.

**The objective should not simply be to remove repetitive responsibilities from employees. It should be to ensure that the responsibilities replacing them create meaningful opportunities for growth, judgment, and contribution.**

## The Hidden Risk of Becoming More Efficient

One of the more interesting findings emerging from research on AI in the workplace is that productivity improvements do not always result in employees having less work.

Research from UC Berkeley Haas in 2026 examined how employees at a technology company adapted to generative AI over several months. Researchers observed that employees often expanded the scope of their activities, took on additional responsibilities, and worked at a faster pace as AI made more tasks accessible.

Rather than automatically reducing workloads, the technology sometimes encouraged people to do more.

This finding challenges a common assumption about efficiency.

When technology makes work easier, organizations and individuals often respond by expanding what they attempt to accomplish. Tasks that previously seemed too time consuming become reasonable to undertake. Requests that once required prioritization become easier to accept. Additional analyses, documentation, and improvements become possible.

Individually, these decisions may appear productive.

Collectively, they can create an environment where the amount of work continually expands to consume the capacity created by AI.

Imagine a manager who previously received a handful of detailed operational reports each month. With AI, the team can now generate those reports daily, accompanied by additional analysis and recommendations.

The availability of information improves dramatically. But the manager now has more information to review, more recommendations to consider, and potentially more decisions to make.

The original effort has been reduced, but the demand placed on another part of the organization has increased.

This is especially relevant as AI makes producing content, analysis, and recommendations increasingly inexpensive.

When generating work becomes easier, the discipline of deciding what work is worth doing becomes more important.

Leaders will need to become more intentional about priorities, decision rights, and the volume of activity they introduce into their organizations.

Otherwise, we risk creating businesses that are capable of producing extraordinary amounts of work while struggling to determine which of that work actually matters.

The goal of AI transformation should not be to fill every hour of capacity that technology creates.

It should be to improve the relationship between effort, outcomes, and the value delivered to customers and employees.

## From Measuring Activity to Measuring Outcomes

For decades, organizations have relied on measures designed around human execution.

We track utilization, productivity, throughput, time spent on activities, number of cases resolved, documents produced, and projects completed. These measures help leaders understand operational performance and allocate resources.

But as AI begins performing a greater portion of the work, some traditional measures become less meaningful when viewed in isolation.

Consider an implementation team that introduces AI to automate configuration preparation and validation. If the team can now complete twice as many configurations, that may appear to represent a significant improvement.

But what if those configurations require additional customer review? What if downstream teams are not prepared to process the increased volume? What if the number of implementations completed remains unchanged?

The activity measure has improved, but the business outcome may not have changed.

This is why I believe leaders need to rethink how AI productivity is measured.

The starting point should be the outcome the organization is trying to achieve.

For a healthcare implementation team, that might mean reducing the time required to activate a new customer while maintaining implementation quality and customer readiness. For customer support, it might mean improving resolution time and customer experience rather than simply increasing the number of cases investigated.

For clinical documentation, the objective might involve reducing administrative burden while maintaining accurate records and supporting better patient interactions.

The measurement must also account for the human effort that remains after automation.

An AI solution that eliminates ten hours of manual preparation but introduces several hours of review, correction, and exception handling has created a different level of benefit than one that reliably produces outcomes requiring minimal intervention.

This is particularly important as organizations introduce autonomous agents. The cost of execution may decrease while the need for oversight, governance, and continuous improvement increases.

Those activities should not be ignored simply because they are less visible than the work being automated.

I would encourage leaders to evaluate AI initiatives through a broader combination of process performance, quality, customer outcomes, human effort, and business value.

This changes the conversation from how much time AI saves to what the organization can accomplish differently because that time is no longer required.

And that is a far more meaningful measure of transformation.

## Leadership Must Decide Where the Capacity Goes

As I think about the organizational implications of successful AI implementation, I believe one of the most important decisions leaders will face is how to allocate the capacity technology creates.

There is no universal answer.

An organization experiencing strong customer demand may choose to use additional capacity to expand delivery without increasing its workforce at the same rate. Another organization may invest in improving service quality, reducing implementation delays, or addressing operational challenges that previously received insufficient attention.

In healthcare, additional capacity could support stronger patient engagement, more effective care coordination, or reduced administrative pressure on clinicians. In customer operations, it could create opportunities for more proactive engagement, better problem prevention, and improved customer experiences.

Other organizations may choose to capture financial efficiencies by reducing the resources required for certain activities. That possibility should not be excluded from the conversation, particularly when automation fundamentally changes the amount of human effort needed to deliver a service.

But these decisions should be made deliberately rather than allowing workforce changes to emerge as an unintended consequence of technology adoption.

The important distinction is that capacity represents an organizational resource, not an automatic outcome.

Just as leaders make decisions about financial investments, technology priorities, and workforce planning, they need to make explicit decisions about how the benefits of AI are reinvested.

This may also require reconsidering organizational boundaries.

When AI reduces the effort required for certain activities, some responsibilities may naturally move closer to the teams that depend on them. A customer implementation specialist may be able to perform validations that previously required a separate technical function. A support professional may be able to resolve issues that previously required escalation.

These changes could reduce handoffs and accelerate outcomes, but they also introduce questions about expertise, access, accountability, and quality.

Leaders should not assume that because AI makes an activity technically possible, it should automatically become part of someone's responsibilities.

The decision should be based on whether the new operating model improves the overall outcome while maintaining appropriate controls.

This is where successful AI implementation becomes an exercise in organizational design.

The technology creates new possibilities, but leadership must determine which possibilities are worth pursuing.

## The Next Evolution of Management

Much of traditional management has been built around coordinating human effort.

Managers establish priorities, allocate responsibilities, monitor progress, remove obstacles, evaluate performance, and develop their teams. These responsibilities remain important, but AI is beginning to change the nature of the work being managed.

As intelligent systems take responsibility for more operational execution, managers will increasingly oversee workflows involving both people and AI agents.

Their attention will need to shift toward understanding how work moves through the system, where judgment is required, how exceptions are resolved, and whether the overall process delivers the expected outcome.

This does not mean managers need to become AI engineers.

It means they need sufficient understanding of intelligent systems to make informed decisions about how work should be designed and governed.

They will need to recognize when automation is appropriate, when human approval remains necessary, and when an existing process should be redesigned rather than simply accelerated.

They will also need to become more effective at managing change.

Employees may experience the removal of familiar responsibilities as an opportunity, a source of uncertainty, or both. Some will welcome the chance to focus on more complex work. Others may be concerned about losing the activities through which they developed expertise and demonstrated their value.

A successful transition requires leaders to communicate how responsibilities are changing, provide opportunities for employees to develop, and ensure that performance expectations evolve alongside the work.

This is particularly important because the benefits of AI may not be distributed evenly across a team.

Some employees may find that their roles become more strategic and engaging. Others may experience increased supervision responsibilities, more complex exceptions, or pressure to manage greater volumes of work.

Without deliberate attention, AI could improve the productivity of the organization while making the experience of work more difficult for some of its people.

The responsibility of leadership is to understand these consequences and manage them thoughtfully.

**The manager of the future will not be measured only by how effectively people complete assigned tasks, but by how intelligently work is distributed, executed, and improved across people and technology.**

## The Real Transformation Begins After Implementation

There is a tendency to treat the successful deployment of AI as the conclusion of a transformation initiative.

The technology has been introduced, employees have been trained, adoption targets have been achieved, and productivity improvements have been demonstrated. The project is considered successful, and attention moves toward the next opportunity.

But I believe this is often the point where the more consequential transformation should begin.

Once AI demonstrates that work can be completed differently, leaders have an opportunity to reconsider the assumptions on which existing processes and organizational structures were built.

Why does this activity require three approvals? Why does information move through multiple teams before a decision can be made? Why are employees evaluated based on activities that intelligent systems now perform? Why does a customer implementation follow the same sequence when parts of that sequence can be completed simultaneously?

These questions go beyond technology adoption.

They challenge the design of the organization itself.

Research from McKinsey has consistently highlighted the importance of workflow redesign among organizations achieving stronger results from AI. The implication is that value does not come exclusively from introducing more capable technology, but from changing how people, processes, and systems work together.

This distinction matters because AI can make inefficient processes faster without making them better.

Organizations that simply automate existing activities may capture incremental productivity improvements. Those willing to reconsider workflows, responsibilities, and decision structures have an opportunity to achieve more fundamental operational change.

I believe this is where the next competitive advantage will emerge.

As AI capabilities become increasingly accessible, the technology itself may offer less differentiation than an organization's ability to redesign work around those capabilities.

The advantage will belong to organizations that can turn productivity into capacity, capacity into better operating models, and those operating models into meaningful outcomes.

## AI Is Working. Now Leadership Has Work to Do.

We are entering a period where successful AI implementation will increasingly become an expectation rather than an achievement that distinguishes an organization.

Intelligent assistants will continue improving. AI agents will execute more complex activities, and automation will become embedded in the applications and processes employees use every day.

The question will no longer be whether AI can help people become more productive.

It will be what organizations choose to do with that productivity.

Some will use AI to accelerate existing processes while leaving their organizational structures largely unchanged. Others will recognize that the reduction in manual effort creates an opportunity to reconsider how responsibilities are distributed, how employees develop, how performance is measured, and how value is delivered.

The difference will not be determined by technology alone.

It will depend on leadership's willingness to challenge established ways of working and make deliberate decisions about the future of the organization.

This includes acknowledging that some responsibilities will become less necessary, others will grow in importance, and new capabilities will need to be developed. It means recognizing that improved productivity can introduce new bottlenecks and that additional capacity can become additional work if priorities are not managed carefully.

Above all, it requires leaders to stop treating time saved as the final measure of success.

The purpose of AI should not simply be to reduce the effort required to accomplish yesterday's work.

It should be to create the capacity to accomplish things that were previously difficult, impractical, or impossible.

We have spent years preparing people to work with artificial intelligence. We are now approaching a moment where we must prepare our organizations to operate differently because of it.

And that brings us to what I believe is one of the most important questions leaders should be asking.

**If AI successfully gives your team back 30% of its capacity tomorrow, do you have a clear vision for what that team should become?**

Because the real transformation does not happen when AI starts doing the work.

**It happens when leaders decide what the organization will do differently because of it.**
