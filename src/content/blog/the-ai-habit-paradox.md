*Why the habits leaders encourage today will determine the intelligence, judgment, and adaptability of their organizations tomorrow.*

In January 2002, Steve Jobs sent a short email to Apple employees containing a quote commonly attributed to Aristotle: "We are what we repeatedly do. Excellence, then, is not an act, but a habit."

The familiar wording was actually written by philosopher and historian Will Durant, interpreting Aristotle's ideas about character and excellence. But the underlying principle remains as relevant today as it was more than two thousand years ago.

What we repeatedly do shapes what we become.

We develop expertise through practice, judgment through experience, and confidence through repeatedly confronting situations that challenge our understanding. Over time, these behaviours become habits, and those habits influence not only our individual capabilities but also the cultures and organizations we build.

Artificial intelligence introduces an interesting complication to this relationship.

We are now introducing technology capable of performing many of the activities through which people traditionally developed their expertise. Writing, analyzing, researching, designing, troubleshooting, and even aspects of decision making can increasingly be supported or executed by intelligent systems.

Organizations are encouraging employees to use these capabilities more frequently. Leaders are celebrating adoption, measuring productivity improvements, and recognizing teams that find new opportunities to automate work.

And understandably so. AI represents an extraordinary opportunity to improve how organizations operate.

But as we make AI part of our daily routines, I believe there is a more fundamental leadership question that deserves our attention.

**If we become what we repeatedly do, what kind of organization are we building when AI increasingly performs the activities through which our people develop their capabilities?**

The answer is not as straightforward as the productivity improvements might suggest.

An organization can become significantly more efficient while gradually reducing the opportunities employees have to develop independent judgment. Teams can produce more sophisticated outputs without necessarily improving their understanding of the underlying problems. And leaders can encourage AI adoption so successfully that they inadvertently create new forms of dependency.

This is what I would describe as the AI Habit Paradox.

**The same technology that makes an organization more capable today could weaken some of the capabilities it will depend on tomorrow, unless leaders deliberately shape how AI becomes part of everyday work.**

## The Habits We Build Become the Organization We Lead

When we think about organizational culture, we often focus on values, leadership principles, communication, and the behaviours we expect employees to demonstrate.

Companies invest considerable effort in defining these expectations. They develop leadership frameworks, establish performance objectives, and communicate the principles that should guide decisions across the organization.

Yet culture is rarely determined by what appears in a strategy document.

It is shaped by what happens repeatedly.

An organization that consistently rewards collaboration will gradually develop more collaborative behaviours. One that prioritizes speed over quality may eventually create a culture where employees feel pressure to deliver quickly, even when additional reflection would produce a better outcome.

The same principle applies to innovation, accountability, customer experience, and professional development.

What leaders consistently encourage, recognize, and reward eventually becomes part of how the organization operates.

Artificial intelligence is now entering this equation.

When leaders encourage employees to use AI for every possible task, they are doing more than promoting technology adoption. They are influencing how employees approach problems, acquire knowledge, and make decisions.

Consider two organizations implementing similar AI capabilities.

In the first, success is primarily measured through adoption rates, time saved, and the volume of work produced. Employees quickly discover that the fastest way to demonstrate value is to delegate as much as possible to intelligent systems. AI generates the analysis, prepares the recommendations, and produces the final deliverables.

In the second organization, productivity remains important, but leaders also emphasize understanding, verification, and learning. Employees are encouraged to use AI to explore alternatives, challenge assumptions, identify weaknesses in their reasoning, and develop better solutions.

Both organizations may achieve significant efficiency improvements.

But over time, they may develop very different capabilities.

The first becomes increasingly proficient at obtaining answers from AI. The second becomes increasingly proficient at using AI to improve how people think and solve problems.

Neither approach is universally appropriate. There are many activities where delegating execution entirely to AI is the right decision.

The difference is whether leaders understand which behaviours they are reinforcing and what those behaviours mean for the organization's future.

**AI adoption is not simply changing how employees complete their work. It is gradually shaping how they approach the work itself.**

And that makes the habits surrounding AI a leadership responsibility.

## The AI Habit Paradox

For generations, the development of professional expertise has depended on repetition.

A software developer learns to recognize architectural weaknesses by designing systems, reviewing code, and resolving difficult technical problems. A project manager develops judgment by navigating dependencies, managing competing priorities, and learning from delivery challenges. A healthcare professional develops expertise through education, supervised practice, clinical experience, and repeated exposure to complex situations.

Not every activity is equally valuable, and repetition alone does not guarantee improvement. But meaningful practice, particularly when accompanied by feedback and reflection, has historically played an important role in developing professional capability.

AI is beginning to change the relationship between performing work and developing expertise.

Consider a technical professional who previously spent several hours investigating an integration issue. The process might involve reviewing documentation, examining logs, comparing system behaviour, testing assumptions, and gradually identifying the underlying cause.

Today, an intelligent system may be able to analyze the available information, identify likely causes, and recommend corrective actions in minutes.

The productivity improvement is obvious.

But what happens if the professional consistently accepts those recommendations without understanding how the conclusion was reached?

The immediate problem may be resolved more efficiently, while the opportunity to develop the reasoning required to solve similar problems independently becomes smaller.

Research is beginning to highlight this distinction.

A 2025 study conducted by Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon University examined how knowledge workers engaged in critical thinking when using generative AI. The researchers observed that greater confidence in AI was associated with less reported critical thinking during AI assisted activities, while the nature of human effort increasingly shifted toward verification and oversight.

Similarly, research published by Anthropic in January 2026 examined developers learning an unfamiliar programming library. Participants who relied heavily on AI assistance completed tasks with greater support but demonstrated weaker understanding in subsequent assessments than those who worked without that assistance. The study also suggested that how participants interacted with AI mattered, particularly when they used it to ask questions and deepen their understanding rather than simply delegate execution.

These studies do not establish that AI inevitably reduces human intelligence or professional expertise. Their findings are more nuanced, and the long term consequences remain uncertain.

But they raise an important leadership concern.

**Completing a task successfully with AI is not the same as developing the capability to understand, evaluate, or perform that task independently.**

This distinction becomes increasingly important as organizations move from AI assistants toward autonomous agents.

When AI simply helps an employee prepare an initial draft, the employee may still participate in much of the underlying work. But when intelligent systems begin executing entire workflows, employees may interact primarily with the final outcome or the exceptions requiring intervention.

The opportunities to learn through execution can become less frequent.

And if we do not deliberately reconsider how employees develop their expertise, we may eventually discover that our organizations have become dependent on systems whose decisions fewer people are capable of meaningfully challenging.

## The Difference Between Delegating Effort and Delegating Judgment

I believe one of the most important responsibilities of AI leadership is understanding the distinction between effort that should be eliminated and judgment that must continue to develop.

Not every task deserves human attention.

There is little value in asking employees to manually consolidate information, format repetitive documentation, or perform routine data transformations simply to preserve the experience of doing those activities.

If AI can execute that work reliably, securely, and efficiently, organizations should take advantage of the opportunity.

But not every activity represents unnecessary effort.

Some activities contribute directly to the development of expertise, critical thinking, professional intuition, and sound judgment.

Consider the preparation of a system architecture proposal.

AI can help analyze requirements, identify integration patterns, recommend technologies, and prepare detailed architectural documentation. These capabilities can significantly accelerate the design process.

However, the value of an experienced architect extends beyond producing a technically plausible design.

It includes understanding the organizational context, recognizing operational constraints, evaluating tradeoffs, anticipating failure scenarios, and determining whether the proposed architecture is appropriate for the business.

If a junior architect consistently delegates the entire design process to AI and participates only in reviewing the completed document, that person may gain experience evaluating outputs without developing the same depth of understanding required to create and defend architectural decisions independently.

This does not mean AI should be excluded from the design process.

It means the interaction should be structured differently depending on whether the objective is execution or capability development.

An experienced architect working under time constraints may appropriately delegate significant portions of the preparation to AI. Someone developing architectural expertise may benefit from forming an initial design, explaining the reasoning behind it, and then using AI to identify weaknesses, propose alternatives, and challenge assumptions.

The technology is the same.

The learning outcome can be very different.

This is where leaders need to become more intentional.

We should encourage teams to delegate activities that consume effort without contributing meaningful value. But we should also identify the experiences that develop the capabilities the organization will need in the future.

**The objective is not to preserve manual work. It is to preserve the development of human expertise while removing the effort that prevents people from applying it effectively.**

## When AI Becomes the First Answer

One of the most subtle changes AI introduces is how easily we can obtain a reasonably sophisticated answer.

Historically, when confronted with an unfamiliar problem, we had to invest effort in understanding it. We searched for information, consulted colleagues, considered alternatives, and gradually developed a perspective.

AI can compress much of that process into a single interaction.

We describe the situation, provide some context, and receive an organized response that may include analysis, recommendations, and proposed next steps.

This is enormously useful.

But it can also change where our thinking begins.

Instead of approaching a problem by asking ourselves what we know, what we do not know, and which assumptions should be examined, we may begin by asking AI what we should think.

The distinction may appear small, but repeated over time it can influence how people develop confidence in their own judgment.

Imagine a manager preparing for a difficult organizational decision.

The manager could ask AI to recommend the best approach and then review the proposed answer. Alternatively, the manager could first articulate an initial perspective, identify the assumptions behind it, and use AI to examine competing interpretations and potential consequences.

Both approaches benefit from AI.

But the second creates a more deliberate opportunity to exercise judgment.

It also encourages a different relationship with the technology.

AI becomes a mechanism for intellectual challenge rather than simply a source of conclusions.

This is particularly important for leadership development.

Leaders are frequently required to make decisions where information is incomplete, competing objectives cannot all be satisfied, and there is no universally correct answer.

AI can provide useful analysis, identify risks, and broaden the range of perspectives considered. But the responsibility for making the decision, understanding its consequences, and remaining accountable for the outcome belongs to the leader.

If we become accustomed to receiving recommendations before developing our own perspective, we risk weakening the habits that help us navigate uncertainty.

The goal should not be to avoid AI recommendations. It should be to remain active participants in the reasoning that determines whether those recommendations are appropriate.

**The danger is not that AI provides answers too easily. It is that we may gradually stop asking ourselves the questions that develop our judgment.**

## The Leadership Habits That Shape AI First Organizations

If organizational behaviour is shaped through repetition, leaders have an opportunity to influence the habits that develop around AI.

I believe three practices are particularly important.

The first is creating a culture where employees are encouraged to challenge AI rather than simply accept it.

As intelligent systems become more capable, their outputs will often appear convincing. A well structured recommendation, a detailed technical explanation, or a comprehensive analysis can create confidence even when important assumptions are incorrect or relevant context is missing.

Leaders need to normalize the expectation that AI outputs should be evaluated according to their significance and risk.

For routine activities, this may involve automated validation and occasional sampling. For important business decisions, it may require examining the assumptions, identifying alternative interpretations, and understanding the evidence supporting a recommendation.

The objective is not to introduce unnecessary friction into every interaction. It is to establish the habit of applying judgment where the consequences justify it.

The second practice is ensuring that learning remains part of execution.

As AI assumes responsibility for more activities, organizations need to reconsider how people acquire the experience required to become experts.

A technical team that relies on AI for troubleshooting might regularly review complex cases and examine why particular solutions were recommended. A customer implementation team might use AI generated configurations as opportunities to understand architectural dependencies and business rules. A leadership team might review significant AI supported decisions to examine which assumptions proved correct and where judgment could improve.

These practices allow organizations to benefit from automation while continuing to build the knowledge required to supervise and improve intelligent systems.

The third practice is changing what leaders recognize as success.

If employees are primarily rewarded for completing more work in less time, it is reasonable to expect them to maximize the amount of execution delegated to AI.

But if leaders also recognize improved judgment, better problem solving, thoughtful experimentation, and the development of reusable knowledge, they encourage a different set of behaviours.

An employee who identifies a weakness in an AI generated recommendation may have created more value than one who produced dozens of outputs without questioning them.

A team that converts an isolated AI experiment into a reliable process may create greater organizational value than one that simply demonstrates frequent use of intelligent tools.

What we measure and reward influences what employees repeat.

And what employees repeat eventually shapes the organization's capabilities.

## The AI Habit Loop

I would describe this relationship through a simple concept: the AI Habit Loop.

It begins with intention.

Every interaction with AI starts with an objective, whether that objective is to complete a task, solve a problem, make a decision, or learn something new.

That intention influences how we interact with the technology.

When the objective is simply to produce an output as quickly as possible, we are likely to delegate most of the effort. When the objective includes developing understanding, we may ask AI to explain its reasoning, challenge our assumptions, examine alternatives, or evaluate our work.

As these interactions are repeated, they become habits.

Employees develop familiar ways of approaching problems. Teams establish expectations about when AI should be used and how its outputs should be evaluated. Managers begin reinforcing particular behaviours through feedback and performance discussions.

Over time, these habits influence capability.

Some skills become stronger because employees continue exercising them. Others may receive less practice because the activities that once developed them have been delegated.

Those changing capabilities then influence how people approach the next interaction with AI, reinforcing the cycle.

The important point is that the loop can produce different outcomes depending on how it is designed.

An organization can reinforce habits of intellectual dependency, where employees increasingly rely on AI to interpret problems and determine appropriate responses.

Or it can develop habits of intelligent collaboration, where AI expands the organization's ability to explore complex problems while employees continue strengthening the judgment required to evaluate and act on the results.

This is why I believe AI First leadership should be concerned with more than adoption.

Adoption tells us whether people are using the technology.

The habits surrounding adoption help determine what the organization is becoming because of it.

## Healthcare Makes the Consequences More Visible

Healthcare provides an important example because professional capability, accountability, and judgment have direct implications for safety and quality.

Consider a clinician using AI to help review patient information, summarize records, or identify potential inconsistencies.

These applications can reduce administrative effort and improve access to relevant information. In complex clinical environments, being able to identify important details more efficiently can be valuable.

But the way these capabilities are introduced matters.

If professionals become accustomed to accepting AI generated summaries without examining their completeness or understanding their limitations, important information could be overlooked. Conversely, if AI is used to identify gaps, surface relevant evidence, and support structured review, it can help professionals exercise judgment more effectively.

The goal is not to require clinicians to repeat administrative work that technology can safely perform.

It is to ensure that clinical reasoning, professional accountability, and appropriate verification remain central to decisions that affect patient care.

A similar principle applies to the teams responsible for implementing and supporting healthcare technology.

Imagine an AI agent capable of preparing configuration instructions for a new customer based on approved requirements and existing product documentation.

The agent may dramatically reduce the amount of manual preparation required.

However, implementation professionals still need to understand the operational implications of those configurations, the dependencies between systems, and the consequences of applying incorrect settings.

If AI becomes responsible for most routine configuration work, leaders must create new opportunities for employees to develop the expertise required to manage complex exceptions and validate the resulting outcomes.

Otherwise, the organization may improve its implementation productivity while gradually reducing the number of people capable of independently understanding how its systems should operate.

This illustrates a broader principle.

**The more execution we delegate to intelligent systems, the more deliberately we must develop the expertise required to oversee them.**

In industries where reliability, safety, and accountability are essential, that responsibility becomes even more significant.

## The Next Measure of AI Maturity

Many organizations are beginning to develop increasingly sophisticated approaches to measuring AI maturity.

They evaluate technology adoption, employee proficiency, integration capabilities, governance frameworks, the number of agents deployed, and the operational benefits generated through automation.

These measures are important. They help organizations understand how effectively AI is being introduced and where additional investment may be required.

But I believe they describe only part of the picture.

An organization may demonstrate high AI adoption while its employees become less confident performing certain tasks independently. It may deploy sophisticated agents while struggling to explain how people are developing the expertise required to supervise them. It may generate significant productivity gains without improving how teams learn, solve problems, or make decisions.

These possibilities suggest that AI maturity should also include an understanding of organizational capability.

Are employees becoming better at recognizing when AI is wrong? Are teams developing stronger problem solving skills? Are managers improving their ability to evaluate complex decisions? Is knowledge being captured and shared in ways that strengthen the organization rather than creating dependency on a few individuals or systems?

These are more difficult questions to measure than the number of prompts submitted or hours saved.

But they may become increasingly important indicators of whether an organization's AI transformation is sustainable.

This also creates a challenge for learning and development.

Traditional training approaches often focus on transferring knowledge and measuring whether employees can recall or apply it. In an AI enabled workplace, employees may have continuous access to information and intelligent assistance.

The development objective therefore needs to evolve.

Rather than simply ensuring employees possess information, organizations must help them develop the ability to interpret context, challenge recommendations, evaluate consequences, and exercise judgment in unfamiliar situations.

That does not eliminate the need for foundational knowledge. In many cases, it makes that knowledge even more important because meaningful oversight requires an understanding of the subject being evaluated.

The future of organizational learning should not be defined by how much information employees can retrieve from AI.

It should be defined by how effectively they can use that information to make better decisions and develop deeper expertise.

## What Leaders Repeatedly Do Matters Most

There is an important leadership dimension to this discussion that extends beyond how employees use AI.

Leaders themselves establish many of the habits that shape organizational behaviour.

If leaders regularly accept AI generated recommendations without examining their assumptions, employees may interpret that behaviour as an expectation.

If leaders prioritize speed above thoughtful decision making, teams will naturally optimize for faster outputs.

But when leaders use AI to challenge their thinking, ask better questions, explore alternative perspectives, and openly examine the limitations of the technology, they demonstrate a different relationship with intelligent systems.

This behaviour becomes particularly influential when leaders are making difficult decisions.

AI can help synthesize information, compare alternatives, and identify risks. But leadership judgment remains essential when priorities conflict, consequences are uncertain, and decisions affect people.

The strongest leaders will not necessarily be those who delegate the greatest number of decisions to AI.

They will be those who understand which decisions benefit from intelligent assistance, which require additional human judgment, and how to use technology without surrendering accountability.

And that capability develops through practice.

Just as employees build habits through repeated AI interactions, leaders develop patterns in how they use technology to inform their own decisions.

The question is whether those patterns strengthen or weaken their ability to lead.

This brings us back to the original idea about repetition and excellence.

Organizational culture is not transformed by announcing an AI First strategy. It changes when leaders repeatedly demonstrate the behaviours they expect their teams to adopt.

When curiosity becomes routine, experimentation becomes more natural. When verification is consistently expected, thoughtful oversight becomes part of execution. When learning is recognized alongside productivity, employees have a reason to develop capabilities rather than simply accelerate outputs.

Over time, these behaviours influence what the organization becomes.

## AI Should Make Organizations More Capable, Not Simply More Productive

We are entering a period in which access to sophisticated intelligence will become increasingly common.

AI will continue improving its ability to generate content, analyze information, solve technical problems, and execute complex workflows. Many activities that currently require significant human effort will become easier to delegate.

This represents an extraordinary opportunity.

But I believe the organizations that benefit most will be those that understand the difference between having access to intelligence and developing organizational intelligence.

The first can increasingly be acquired through technology.

The second depends on the capabilities, judgment, knowledge, and behaviours that exist across the organization.

Leaders need to ensure that these two forms of intelligence develop together.

That means encouraging automation where it creates meaningful value while preserving opportunities for employees to learn and grow. It means recognizing that speed and productivity are important, but insufficient measures of success. And it means creating environments where AI is used not only to generate answers, but also to improve the quality of the questions people ask.

We should not be trying to preserve every activity that people perform today. Many of those activities deserve to be automated.

But we should be deliberate about the capabilities we want our organizations to retain and strengthen as the nature of work changes.

Because the habits we establish today will influence the expertise we possess tomorrow.

More than two thousand years after Aristotle explored the relationship between habit and character, we are confronting a new version of the same fundamental idea.

We become what we repeatedly do.

Artificial intelligence does not change that principle. It changes which activities we choose to practice, which responsibilities we delegate, and which capabilities we continue developing.

For leaders, that creates a responsibility extending far beyond technology adoption.

We must begin asking not only what AI can accomplish for our organizations, but what our organizations are becoming through their repeated use of AI.

**The real measure of AI leadership will not be how successfully we teach people to use intelligent systems. It will be whether our people and organizations continue becoming more capable because of them.**

Because in the end, the habits we encourage today will determine the intelligence we can rely on tomorrow.
