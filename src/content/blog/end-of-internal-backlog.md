Someone in Operations needs a dashboard. The data already exists, the question they are trying to answer is understood, and the value of having the information is obvious. Yet somehow, the dashboard takes six weeks.
A request needs to be submitted. Someone needs to determine its priority. Requirements need to be documented. A technical resource needs to become available. The data needs to be connected. The dashboard needs to be built, reviewed and probably changed several times before something useful eventually appears. The strange part is that actually building the dashboard may have taken only a few hours. The organization surrounding the dashboard took six weeks.
We have become so accustomed to this model that we rarely question it. Business teams identify problems and technical teams build solutions. Between those two groups sits an increasingly sophisticated system of intake processes, prioritization frameworks, project queues and backlogs designed primarily to manage scarce technical capacity.
Artificial intelligence is beginning to challenge that assumption. The interesting question is no longer simply how AI can help technical teams build faster. Increasingly, organizations should be asking something much more fundamental: Why did this need to become a technical request in the first place?

## The Distance Between a Problem and a Solution

For most of the history of enterprise technology, creating software required specialized knowledge. If you wanted an application, someone needed to write code. If you wanted a dashboard, someone needed to understand databases and business intelligence tools. If you wanted systems to communicate, someone needed to understand APIs, integrations and infrastructure. That specialization made sense because software was expensive to create, complicated to maintain and potentially dangerous when implemented incorrectly.
But the interface for creating technology has steadily become simpler. We moved from code to configuration, from configuration to visual builders, and eventually toward low code and no code platforms. AI is now taking us into another transition, one where conversation itself increasingly becomes the development interface.
Instead of understanding how an application should be constructed, we can increasingly describe what we want it to accomplish. A leader might ask for a dashboard showing every customer going live in the next 60 days, then ask it to highlight customers with unresolved integration dependencies, separate implementations by region, identify the projects with the greatest risk and create a portfolio view for directors. The solution evolves through conversation rather than through a traditional requirements and development cycle.
That changes more than software development. It dramatically reduces the distance between recognizing an operational problem and creating something that helps solve it.

## Low Code Is Not the Real Story

Low code platforms have existed for years, which is why I do not think low code itself is the most interesting part of what is happening. The more consequential change is that the knowledge required to translate a business problem into software is becoming dramatically smaller.
Historically, business users understood the problem while technical teams understood how to construct the solution. A significant amount of organizational machinery developed around translating between those two worlds. Requirements documents, business analysts, intake processes, development queues and prioritization meetings all became necessary because understanding the problem and building the solution were separate capabilities.
AI increasingly occupies part of that translation layer. A business user can describe an outcome, refine what they want conversationally and increasingly create dashboards, internal tools, workflows and small applications around approved organizational data. The person closest to the problem can participate directly in creating the solution rather than simply describing it to someone else.
That is a much bigger idea than low code. It is the democratization of software creation inside the enterprise.

## From Asking AI to Building With AI

Many organizations are still measuring AI adoption primarily by how frequently employees use AI assistants. That is an important starting point, but it represents only the beginning of what AI fluency will eventually mean. I see the progression happening through five increasingly consequential levels.
At the first level, employees Ask. They use AI to retrieve information, explain concepts, summarize documents and answer questions. At the second level, they Analyze, using AI to interrogate information, compare datasets, identify patterns and generate insights. At the third level, they Build, creating dashboards, internal tools, workflows, collaborative environments and small applications. At the fourth level, they Connect, allowing those solutions to interact with approved enterprise information and systems. Finally, they Operate, where AI agents begin taking governed actions through those environments and software starts participating more directly in the work itself.
The transition from Ask to Build is particularly important. When employees ask AI questions, AI makes individuals more productive. When employees begin building with AI, it starts changing the operating capacity of the organization itself.
That is where the economics become much more interesting.

## Imagine This Inside Healthcare

Healthcare organizations are filled with operational workflows that live between major enterprise systems. These workflows can be incredibly important, yet individually they are often not significant enough to justify dedicated product development. As a result, they frequently become spreadsheets, manually assembled reports, email processes or items sitting somewhere in a technical backlog.
Consider a healthcare implementation organization managing dozens of customer implementations simultaneously. Information may exist across CRM systems, implementation platforms, configuration records, integration systems, meeting notes and customer communications. An implementation leader may simply want to understand which customers are going live in the next 60 days, which still have unresolved configuration activities, which integrations are behind schedule, which customers have not provided required data and which project milestones are beginning to slip.
Historically, answering those questions might require several reports, spreadsheets or a request to a reporting or technical team. Now imagine that leader describing the experience they need and having an initial operational dashboard created. They continue the conversation by asking it to separate pharmacy integrations, highlight implementations where required customer data has not been received, create a director view across the entire portfolio and identify the dependencies responsible for the most delays.
Within hours, the organization could have something useful. It does not necessarily need to become a new enterprise product, nor does it need to be designed to exist for the next decade. It simply needs to solve the operational problem in front of the team.
The same pattern can exist throughout healthcare. A clinical operations team could create a temporary workspace for tracking an initiative. A support leader could create an escalation command centre. A data migration team could create a validation dashboard. An implementation team could create a readiness workspace. A customer operations leader could create a view connecting implementation status, support activity and adoption indicators.
There are thousands of operational gaps between the large systems organizations have already purchased. Historically, many remained unsolved because they were too small to justify competing for technical capacity. AI changes the economics of solving those gaps.

## The Rise of Disposable Software

This leads to an idea I believe organizations will increasingly need to become comfortable with: disposable software.
For most of the software era, applications were expensive enough to create that permanence became part of how we justified building them. If an organization was going to invest months of engineering capacity, the application needed to solve a sufficiently large problem and remain useful for a sufficiently long period of time.
But what happens when creating useful software takes hours rather than months? The economics change. Some internal applications may still exist for years, but others might exist for a quarter, for the duration of a customer implementation, for a transformation program or perhaps even for a single executive meeting. Once the problem disappears, the software can disappear with it.
Disposable does not mean poorly engineered or carelessly governed. It means that permanence no longer needs to be a prerequisite for creation. This creates a very different operating mindset. Instead of asking whether a problem is important enough to justify building an application, teams can begin asking whether a small piece of software would simply make the work easier.
That may sound like a subtle distinction, but at organizational scale it is enormous.

## What Happens to the Backlog?

For years, technology organizations have tried to solve growing demand primarily by increasing delivery capacity. We hire more developers, add analysts, improve prioritization, implement agile methodologies, create better intake processes and continuously optimize the backlog.
AI introduces another possibility. Rather than only increasing how quickly work moves through the backlog, organizations can reduce how much work needs to enter the backlog in the first place.
Imagine hundreds of small requests disappearing from centralized technical queues because employees can safely solve them themselves. A dashboard, a temporary data collection tool, a project tracker, a reporting interface or a small internal application may not individually transform the organization. Collectively, however, these requests represent enormous amounts of friction.
Removing that friction creates something organizations constantly seek but rarely achieve: operational velocity. The organization becomes better at solving small problems while they are still small, rather than allowing them to wait behind larger initiatives.

## The Dangerous Version of This Future

There is, however, an obvious counterargument. If everyone can build software, everyone can also build bad software. The same technology that removes bottlenecks can create an entirely different kind of organizational chaos.
Twenty teams could create twenty dashboards representing the same metric differently. Employees could unknowingly duplicate business logic. Applications could connect to inappropriate datasets. Sensitive healthcare information could appear somewhere it should not. Someone could create an important operational workflow, leave the organization and take the understanding of how that workflow operates with them.
The age of AI generated software could therefore become the next generation of shadow IT, except this time the creation of shadow technology happens dramatically faster.
The answer cannot simply be to let everyone build everything. The better principle is to let everyone build within governed boundaries. The organizations that get this right will democratize creation without democratizing risk.

## Freedom at the Experience Layer

I believe organizations will increasingly need to separate three things that historically have been tightly coupled: the experience, the data and the action.
At the Experience Layer, employees should have increasing freedom to create dashboards, interfaces, workflows and applications that make their work easier. At the Data Layer, the organization must continue governing what information people and AI systems can access, where that information originates and which datasets represent authoritative sources. At the Action Layer, controls should become stronger as AI generated applications move from simply displaying information toward changing enterprise data, triggering workflows or making consequential decisions.
This distinction becomes especially important in healthcare. The goal cannot be unrestricted experimentation with sensitive information. Identity, permissions, privacy, security, auditability and appropriate access to protected health information must remain foundational. But governance should not become an excuse for preventing creation.
The architecture of the AI First enterprise should therefore create a safe environment where employees can experiment and build freely within clearly defined boundaries. Freedom at the Experience Layer. Governance at the Data Layer. Control at the Action Layer.
That balance between democratization and governance may become one of the defining capabilities of an AI First organization.

## Technology Teams Become Builders of Capability

There is another consequence that may be even more important. For decades, organizations operated with a relatively simple relationship: the business requests and technology builds.
That relationship is beginning to change. Technology teams will certainly continue building complex enterprise systems, integrations, platforms and mission critical capabilities. But an increasingly important part of their responsibility will be creating environments where other people can safely build.
That means creating trusted data foundations, providing approved connectors, defining identity and permission models, establishing reusable components, creating governance frameworks and monitoring what is being created. Technical teams increasingly become responsible not only for producing solutions, but for designing the environment in which solutions can safely emerge.
The technology organization therefore begins moving from being only a builder of solutions toward becoming a builder of capability.
The distinction is important. If every business problem still requires a technology team to solve it, AI may make the technology team faster. If the technology team creates an environment where thousands of employees can safely solve smaller problems themselves, AI makes the entire organization faster.
Those are very different outcomes.

## From Technical Capacity to Organizational Capacity

This may ultimately become a more meaningful measure of AI transformation. Organizations frequently ask how much faster their developers are, how many AI tools have been deployed, how many employees are using AI and how many hours have been saved. These are useful questions, but they largely measure the efficiency of existing work.
Perhaps another question should begin appearing in leadership conversations: How many problems can our organization now solve without creating another dependency?
That is organizational capacity.
The organizations that move fastest may not necessarily be those with the largest technology teams. They may be the ones where the ability to create technology has been distributed most effectively. Employees closest to customers can solve customer problems. Operations teams can create operational tools. Analysts can turn insights into experiences. Leaders can create the information environments they need to make decisions. Technology teams provide the architecture and guardrails that allow all of this to happen safely.
The result is not simply a more productive technology organization. It is a more adaptable enterprise.

## The End of the Internal Backlog

The internal backlog is not actually going to disappear. Complex problems will continue to require skilled engineers. Enterprise platforms will continue to require rigorous architecture. Healthcare systems will continue to demand extraordinary levels of security, reliability and governance.
But something important is changing underneath them. Thousands of smaller operational problems that once waited for technical capacity may no longer need to wait. The person who understands the problem may increasingly be able to create the first version of the solution themselves.
That changes the relationship between people and technology. It changes the role of technical organizations. It changes the economics of software. Most importantly, it changes the speed at which an organization can adapt.
For years, we have measured great technology organizations partly by how much they could build for the business. In an AI First organization, we may eventually need to measure something different: How much can the organization safely build without them?
The future of enterprise technology is not simply about giving developers better tools. It is about giving more people the ability to turn ideas into working solutions while giving the organization the governance to make that creation safe.
And when that happens, the most valuable thing a technology organization builds may no longer be the application. It may be the environment that allows everyone else to build.
