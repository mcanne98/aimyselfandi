*Why the next challenge in enterprise AI is not building more agents, but learning how to manage the ones we already have.*

For decades, organizations have invested heavily in understanding how to manage people and technology. We have developed sophisticated approaches to workforce planning, organizational structures, performance management, access controls, governance, and operational accountability. We know that when someone joins an organization, they need a defined role, clear responsibilities, appropriate permissions, training, and someone accountable for their performance.

Yet as we enter the era of agentic artificial intelligence, we are beginning to introduce a new category of operational resources into our organizations without necessarily applying the same management discipline.

AI agents are no longer limited to answering questions or generating content. They are increasingly capable of retrieving information, interacting with enterprise applications, making decisions within defined boundaries, executing workflows, and coordinating activities across multiple systems. What began as an intelligent assistant is gradually becoming an active participant in how organizations operate.

And this creates a leadership challenge that I believe we are significantly underestimating.

We are becoming remarkably good at building AI agents. The tools are becoming more accessible, the technical barriers are decreasing, and employees across business functions can now experiment with capabilities that previously required dedicated engineering teams. But our ability to create agents is advancing faster than our ability to manage them.

The result could be an organization with hundreds of intelligent agents executing work, accessing sensitive information, and influencing business outcomes, without a clear understanding of who owns them, how they interact, or whether they are still operating as intended.

**We would never hire hundreds of employees without knowing who manages them, what they are responsible for, how they perform, or what authority they have. Yet we are beginning to do something remarkably similar with AI agents.**

This is why I believe the next major challenge in enterprise AI will not be agent development. It will be Digital Workforce Management.

## The Agent Explosion Is Creating a New Organizational Reality

The adoption of AI agents is accelerating, and the early evidence suggests that organizations are already experiencing the consequences of deploying intelligent systems faster than they can establish effective governance.

Research published by G2 in October 2026 found that 38% of surveyed organizations operating AI agents in production had more than 100 agents deployed. Yet only 27% maintained a central registry of their agents, while 47% reported informal or decentralized ownership and controls.

These findings illustrate an important imbalance. Organizations are increasing the number of intelligent systems performing operational activities without necessarily developing a comprehensive view of what those systems are doing.

Historically, enterprise software has been managed through relatively established disciplines. Applications are registered, infrastructure is monitored, security permissions are assigned, changes are controlled, and incidents are investigated. Even when these practices are imperfect, organizations generally understand the need for them.

AI agents introduce a different level of complexity because they do not simply store information or respond to predefined transactions. They can interpret context, select actions, interact with other systems, and influence the sequence of activities required to achieve an outcome.

Consider an agent designed to support customer onboarding. It may retrieve information from a customer relationship management platform, interpret implementation requirements, generate configuration instructions, coordinate with technical teams, and update project records. Each activity may be appropriate individually, but the agent's overall behaviour depends on the information it receives, the permissions it holds, and the decisions it makes along the way.

Now imagine dozens of similar agents operating across Sales, Professional Services, Customer Support, Finance, and Operations.

The complexity no longer comes from the number of applications alone. It comes from the number of intelligent actors operating within those applications, potentially influencing one another's decisions and outcomes.

This is where the traditional view of AI as another technology deployment begins to break down.

**The moment an intelligent system moves from providing information to executing work, it becomes part of the organization's operating model.**

And anything that becomes part of the operating model requires management.

## We Are Managing Agents Like Software, When They Behave More Like Operational Resources

There is an important distinction between deploying software and delegating responsibility.

Traditional software typically operates through predefined workflows. A user initiates an action, the application executes the relevant logic, and the resulting transaction follows an established sequence. While software can certainly be complex and automated, its operational boundaries are generally designed around specific functions.

AI agents introduce a more dynamic relationship between instructions, context, reasoning, and execution. An agent may determine which tools to use, what information to retrieve, how to approach a problem, and which actions to perform within the authority it has been granted.

This does not make an agent equivalent to a human employee. Agents do not possess human judgment, professional responsibility, or an independent understanding of organizational consequences. Their capabilities remain dependent on their design, the systems they access, and the quality of the context available to them.

But from an operational perspective, they increasingly perform activities that organizations have historically assigned to people.

This is why the employee analogy becomes useful.

When an employee joins an organization, we establish their responsibilities, assign a manager, provide access to the systems they need, define performance expectations, and create mechanisms for reviewing their work. As responsibilities evolve, their permissions, training, and expectations are adjusted.

Now consider how many organizations approach AI agents. A team identifies an opportunity, develops an agent, connects it to the necessary applications, tests a few scenarios, and releases it into production. The agent begins generating value, and attention quickly shifts toward the next opportunity.

What happens six months later?

Has the underlying business process changed? Are the instructions still accurate? Does the agent retain permissions it no longer requires? Has its performance deteriorated because the data it relies on has changed? Is anyone reviewing the exceptions it encounters or the decisions it makes?

These are not simply technical questions. They are operational management questions.

The difference is that an employee can recognize ambiguity, seek clarification, challenge instructions, and communicate concerns through established organizational relationships. An agent may continue executing within its configured boundaries even when those boundaries no longer reflect the organization's intentions.

That makes deliberate oversight even more important.

**An AI agent should not be considered successfully deployed simply because it works. It should be considered operationally ready when the organization can demonstrate that it remains controlled, accountable, measurable, and appropriate for the work it performs.**

## Every Agent Needs an Accountable Owner

One of the most important questions organizations need to answer is surprisingly simple: Who is responsible for this agent?

Not who developed it. Not who maintains the infrastructure. Not which technology platform hosts it.

Who is accountable for the business outcomes it produces?

In traditional organizations, accountability is generally attached to roles and functions. A Finance leader is responsible for financial processes. A Customer Support leader is responsible for service outcomes. A Professional Services leader is responsible for implementation delivery.

Introducing AI agents should not dissolve those responsibilities.

If an agent processes customer information incorrectly, executes an inappropriate configuration, or makes a decision outside its intended authority, the organization needs to know who is responsible for investigating the issue, correcting the outcome, and preventing recurrence.

The answer cannot simply be that the agent made a mistake.

AI agents can execute work, but accountability remains with the organization and the people responsible for the processes in which those agents operate.

This distinction becomes especially important when agents cross functional boundaries.

Imagine an agent that supports a customer implementation by gathering commercial requirements from Salesforce, interpreting a Statement of Work, preparing configuration instructions, and coordinating technical validation. The agent may interact with systems owned by Sales, Professional Services, Product, and Technical Services.

If the commercial requirements conflict with the configuration standards, who determines which instruction takes precedence? If an exception requires customer approval, which team is responsible for obtaining it? If the agent proceeds with an incorrect assumption, who owns the resulting issue?

Without explicit accountability, these situations can quickly become organizational gaps.

I believe every production agent should have a clearly identified business owner who is accountable for its purpose, performance, authority, and continued relevance. Technical teams remain essential for maintaining the infrastructure, security, and reliability of the agent, but business accountability should remain connected to the function receiving the value and bearing the operational consequences.

This creates a useful separation between technical ownership and operational ownership.

The technology team ensures that the agent can operate safely and reliably. The business owner ensures that it is performing the right work, following the right rules, and delivering the intended outcomes.

Both are necessary, but they are not interchangeable.

## The Digital Workforce Needs an Operational Lifecycle

Organizations have established processes for bringing employees into the business, supporting their development, evaluating performance, changing responsibilities, and managing departures.

We need to begin thinking about AI agents through a similarly disciplined lifecycle.

This starts before an agent is deployed.

Organizations should understand why the agent exists, what business problem it is expected to solve, which activities it is authorized to perform, and how success will be measured. These expectations should be explicit rather than buried in prompts, configuration settings, or technical documentation.

The next consideration is identity and access. An agent should have a distinct identity wherever the underlying systems support it, with permissions appropriate to its responsibilities. It should not inherit broad access simply because connecting it through an existing employee account is easier.

As agents become more capable of interacting with multiple systems, access management becomes increasingly important. An agent that can retrieve information, modify records, trigger workflows, and communicate externally represents a different level of operational risk from one that simply summarizes documents.

Once deployed, the agent needs ongoing evaluation.

Its performance should be measured not only through technical indicators such as response time or execution success, but also through business outcomes. Did it complete the intended process correctly? How often did it require human intervention? Were exceptions handled appropriately? Did it create additional work elsewhere in the organization?

Organizations should also monitor changes in the environment in which the agent operates. Business rules evolve, applications change, knowledge becomes outdated, and new compliance requirements emerge. An agent that performed effectively when first deployed may gradually become less reliable if these changes are not reflected in its instructions and supporting context.

Finally, agents need a retirement process.

An agent may become redundant when a business process changes, a more capable solution becomes available, or its responsibilities are absorbed into another workflow. At that point, its permissions should be removed, integrations disconnected, and operational records preserved according to the organization's requirements.

Without this discipline, organizations risk accumulating agents that remain active long after their original purpose has disappeared.

This is the AI equivalent of operational sprawl, but with an important difference. These resources may continue taking actions rather than simply consuming infrastructure.

The challenge is not to create bureaucracy around every experiment. Early prototypes should remain easy to develop and test. The discipline should increase as agents gain access to sensitive information, receive authority to execute actions, and become embedded in critical business processes.

**The greater the autonomy granted to an agent, the greater the operational discipline required to manage it.**

## Healthcare Illustrates Why Agent Management Matters

Healthcare provides a particularly compelling example because the consequences of incorrect information, inappropriate access, or poorly coordinated workflows can extend well beyond operational inefficiency.

Consider the implementation of a new healthcare organization onto an electronic health record platform.

The process involves multiple interconnected activities, including collecting facility information, understanding operational requirements, preparing configurations, validating data, establishing integrations, coordinating training, and confirming readiness for activation.

Today, many of these activities depend on people coordinating information across systems and teams. AI agents could eventually take responsibility for significant portions of this work, reducing repetitive effort and improving consistency.

An implementation agent might interpret approved requirements and prepare configuration instructions. A data validation agent might identify discrepancies in migration files. An integration agent might evaluate connectivity requirements and confirm technical readiness. A training agent might assemble relevant learning materials based on the customer's enabled products and configuration.

Individually, these agents could provide meaningful value.

But the real complexity emerges when their activities become interconnected.

Imagine the data validation agent identifies a discrepancy in facility information after the implementation agent has already prepared configuration instructions. The discrepancy could affect integration mappings, user permissions, and activation readiness.

Should the implementation agent automatically update the configuration? Should the integration agent pause its work? Does the change require customer confirmation? Who determines whether the issue is significant enough to delay activation?

These questions cannot be answered solely by giving each agent better reasoning capabilities.

They require clearly defined decision authority, shared context, escalation rules, and accountability for the overall implementation outcome.

The same principle applies to clinical workflows, where agents may eventually assist with administrative coordination, information reconciliation, and transitions between care settings. The more closely an agent's actions affect patient information or care delivery, the more important it becomes to establish explicit boundaries and retain appropriate human oversight.

In healthcare, the goal should not be to maximize autonomy at every opportunity. It should be to introduce autonomy where it can improve outcomes without compromising safety, privacy, professional accountability, or trust.

This is why Digital Workforce Management should be considered part of the operational architecture rather than an administrative activity introduced after deployment.

## The Missing Layer Between AI Governance and AI Operations

Many organizations are already developing AI governance frameworks. They are establishing policies around acceptable use, data protection, model selection, security, and responsible AI practices.

These efforts are important, but governance policies alone do not ensure that agents operate effectively.

There is a difference between defining what AI is allowed to do and continuously understanding what AI is actually doing.

A policy may state that agents cannot access certain categories of information. Operational management must ensure those restrictions are reflected in permissions and remain effective as systems change.

A policy may require human approval for sensitive decisions. Operational management must ensure the approval mechanism exists, is consistently enforced, and can be audited.

A policy may establish expectations for accuracy and reliability. Operational management must determine whether the agent continues meeting those expectations over time.

This is where I believe a new management capability begins to emerge.

Digital Workforce Management connects governance with execution. It provides the operational discipline required to manage agents as persistent participants in business processes rather than isolated technology deployments.

That capability could include maintaining an inventory of agents, defining ownership, tracking permissions, monitoring performance, managing exceptions, coordinating changes, and evaluating whether each agent continues to deliver value.

Importantly, I do not believe every organization needs to create a new department to accomplish this.

The responsibility may be distributed across existing business, technology, security, and operational functions. What matters is that the responsibilities are explicit and the information required to manage agents is accessible.

There is also a broader architectural implication.

As organizations introduce multiple agents, they will need mechanisms to understand not only individual agent performance but also how agents interact across workflows. An agent may perform its assigned task correctly while contributing to an undesirable overall outcome because another agent relied on incomplete or outdated information.

This means organizations must evaluate the performance of the system as a whole, not simply the performance of its individual components.

**An organization can have highly effective AI agents and still have an ineffective AI operating model.**

The difference lies in how those agents are coordinated, governed, and managed.

## From Managing Employees and Systems to Managing Intelligent Execution

For much of the modern enterprise, management responsibilities have been divided between people and technology.

Business leaders manage teams, priorities, performance, and outcomes. Technology leaders manage infrastructure, applications, integrations, reliability, and security.

AI agents increasingly operate at the intersection of these responsibilities.

They are technology systems, but they also perform business activities. They require technical maintenance, but their success is measured through operational outcomes. They follow instructions, but those instructions may involve interpreting information and selecting actions rather than executing a fixed sequence of steps.

This creates a management challenge that neither traditional people management nor conventional IT operations fully addresses on its own.

I believe leaders will need to develop a new form of operational fluency: understanding how intelligent execution is designed, delegated, monitored, and improved.

A manager overseeing a customer support function may eventually be responsible for both human specialists and a collection of AI agents handling routine inquiries, retrieving information, and executing approved resolutions.

The manager will need to understand how work is distributed, which activities require human judgment, how exceptions are escalated, and whether the combined system is delivering the expected customer experience.

Similarly, a Professional Services leader may need to manage a delivery model in which intelligent agents prepare documentation, validate configurations, and coordinate implementation activities while employees focus on customer relationships, complex decisions, and accountability for delivery outcomes.

The objective is not to treat agents as people. It is to recognize that managing work increasingly requires oversight of both human and machine execution.

This will also change how organizations think about productivity.

Traditional productivity measures often focus on individual output, utilization, or the time required to complete activities. In an environment where intelligent agents execute substantial portions of operational work, these measures may become less meaningful on their own.

Leaders will need to place greater emphasis on outcomes, quality, reliability, exception rates, customer experience, and the overall cost of delivering a service.

The important question becomes less about how much work an individual employee or agent completes and more about how effectively the entire operating model produces the intended result.

## The Next Competitive Advantage May Be Operational Discipline

There is considerable excitement around the growing accessibility of AI agent development.

Organizations can now experiment with intelligent automation at a pace that would have been difficult to imagine only a few years ago. Employees can identify opportunities, build prototypes, connect tools, and demonstrate value without waiting for lengthy development cycles.

This accessibility is a significant advantage, and organizations should continue encouraging experimentation.

But the ability to build agents will not remain a meaningful competitive differentiator on its own.

As the technology becomes more accessible, the advantage will increasingly shift toward an organization's ability to deploy agents responsibly, integrate them into meaningful workflows, and continuously improve their performance.

This is a familiar pattern in technological transformation.

The early advantage often belongs to those who adopt a technology first. Over time, as the technology becomes more widely available, the advantage shifts toward those who understand how to use it effectively at scale.

With AI agents, that means moving beyond demonstrations of individual capability toward a disciplined operating model for intelligent execution.

An organization with fifty well managed agents delivering measurable business outcomes may be substantially more effective than one with five hundred agents operating without clear ownership or coordination.

The number of agents deployed is not a meaningful measure of transformation if the organization cannot explain what those agents accomplish, how reliably they perform, or who is accountable when something goes wrong.

The objective should not be to maximize the size of the digital workforce. It should be to maximize the value, reliability, and accountability of the work it performs.

## The Workforce We Are Not Yet Prepared to Manage

We are entering a period in which the distinction between software applications, intelligent assistants, and autonomous operational systems will become increasingly important.

Organizations will continue introducing agents into customer operations, finance, technology, healthcare, and many other functions. Some will perform narrow tasks. Others will coordinate complex processes across multiple systems. Over time, the number of intelligent systems participating in everyday business operations could become substantial.

The technology required to build these agents will continue improving. Their capabilities will expand, their integration with enterprise platforms will deepen, and their ability to execute work will become more sophisticated.

But none of that automatically creates an organization capable of managing them.

That responsibility belongs to leadership.

We need to begin asking different questions. Not simply how many agents can we build, but which agents should exist? Not only what can they automate, but who is accountable for their outcomes? Not merely whether they work today, but how we will know they are still working appropriately tomorrow?

These questions may seem operational, but they are fundamentally strategic. They determine whether AI becomes a collection of disconnected automations or a reliable capability embedded into how an organization delivers value.

I believe the organizations that succeed in the next phase of AI will be those that recognize this distinction early.

They will encourage experimentation while establishing clear operational boundaries. They will distribute the ability to create intelligent solutions while maintaining visibility into how those solutions operate. And they will recognize that delegating execution to AI does not mean delegating accountability.

For decades, leadership has involved creating the conditions in which people and technology can work together to achieve outcomes. AI is introducing a new dimension to that responsibility.

**The future of enterprise AI will not be defined by how many agents an organization can deploy. It will be defined by how effectively those agents are managed, how responsibly they are trusted, and how consistently they contribute to meaningful outcomes.**

We are building a digital workforce.

Now we need to learn how to manage it.
