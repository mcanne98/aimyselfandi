For much of the last few years, the AI conversation has been dominated by speed. Models became more capable, organizations raced to adopt copilots, leaders were encouraged to experiment, and increasingly the question became how quickly businesses could move from experimentation into production. There was good reason for that urgency. AI was advancing quickly, and organizations that spent too much time debating its potential risked learning far more slowly than the technology itself was evolving.
But I think we are entering a different phase of that conversation.
Dario Amodei's recent essay, We Must Pace the Frontier, captures an important shift. His argument is not that AI development should stop, nor does he retreat from the extraordinary potential he has previously described for AI across medicine, science, productivity, and economic growth. Instead, he raises a more uncomfortable possibility: AI capability may soon be progressing faster than our ability to understand, evaluate, and control it.
That distinction matters because the nature of AI itself is changing. The systems we were experimenting with several years ago were largely passive. We asked a question and received an answer. We provided a document and received a summary. We described a piece of software and received some code. Humans remained firmly between the intelligence and the action.
That boundary is beginning to disappear.
AI systems are increasingly able to reason across multiple steps, interact with software, use tools, retrieve information, coordinate activities, write and execute code, and operate with decreasing levels of human involvement. We are moving from systems that primarily generate information toward systems that increasingly perform work.
That creates a very different leadership problem.

## The AI Control Gap

I think of it as the AI Control Gap.
The AI Control Gap is the distance between what AI is capable of doing and what an organization is capable of safely allowing it to do. For the past few years, most attention has been placed on the first side of that equation. We have watched the capability curve rise at an extraordinary rate. Reasoning improves. Context windows expand. Agents become more capable. Models become multimodal. The cost of intelligence continues to fall.
The control curve has moved much more slowly.
Most organizations still govern AI using structures created for traditional software. Security reviews, architecture committees, privacy assessments, procurement processes, access controls, and human approvals remain important, but they were designed for systems whose behaviour changed largely when humans changed the software.
AI introduces something different. The system itself is becoming increasingly capable of interpreting context and determining how work should be performed. As agents become connected to enterprise systems, the question is no longer simply whether the model produces a good answer. The question becomes what authority the system has been given, what information it can access, which actions it can perform, what happens when it encounters ambiguity, and how the organization understands what happened afterward.

## AI Building AI

This is where Amodei's argument becomes particularly relevant beyond the frontier AI labs.
One of the developments he highlights is the increasing role AI plays in building AI itself. Models already assist with coding, research, experimentation, evaluation, and parts of the model development process. As those capabilities improve, AI may contribute more meaningfully to creating the next generation of AI systems. That introduces a feedback loop that is fundamentally different from previous technology cycles.
Imagine an engineer building a machine. Then imagine that machine helping the engineer build the next version. That next version becomes better at helping build the version after that. Each generation potentially compresses the development cycle further.
Eventually, the constraint may no longer be how quickly humans can build intelligence. It may become how quickly humans can understand what that intelligence is capable of doing.
That is where the idea of pacing becomes important.
But I would extend Amodei's argument in one important direction. The challenge is not only that frontier models may advance too quickly. There is already a significant gap between the AI capabilities available today and the ability of many organizations to operationalize them responsibly.
Even if frontier AI development slowed tomorrow, that gap would still exist.
AI capabilities are already moving faster than many organizational operating models. They are moving faster than workforce skills, governance structures, cybersecurity practices, data architecture, procurement models, and in some cases leadership understanding. The constraint is increasingly not whether AI can perform a task. It is whether the organization is ready to delegate that task to AI.

## Healthcare Makes This Visible

Healthcare makes this distinction particularly visible.
Consider the evolution of a relatively simple clinical workflow. An AI system summarizes a resident record for a clinician. There is value in that capability, but the human remains responsible for interpreting the information and determining what happens next.
Now imagine the system identifies a potential change in condition. It recognizes a pattern across clinical documentation, medications, observations, and recent events that may deserve attention. The value increases, but so does the responsibility being placed on the system.
Take the next step. The AI identifies the change, retrieves the relevant clinical context, determines who should be notified, prepares the communication, updates documentation, creates follow up activities, and coordinates the workflow across members of the care team.
We have moved from information retrieval to workflow orchestration.
The intelligence may be capable of doing all of those things. But capability alone does not tell us whether it should.
This is why I believe healthcare organizations will increasingly need to think about AI governance as an operational discipline rather than primarily as a compliance exercise. The important question will not simply be whether an AI model is accurate. Organizations will need to understand what the system can observe, what it can recommend, what it can decide, what it can execute, and under what circumstances a human needs to intervene.
For healthcare technology platforms, this becomes particularly important because the potential of AI grows as intelligence becomes connected to more context and more workflows. A platform that connects clinical information, care teams, providers, pharmacies, hospitals, payers, and other participants across the healthcare ecosystem creates an extraordinary foundation for intelligent workflows.
The opportunity is to move beyond simply presenting information toward helping people understand what matters and what should happen next. AI can surface the right context, reduce the burden of navigating fragmented information, assist with documentation, support coordination, and eventually help orchestrate increasingly complex workflows.
But the more connected the intelligence becomes, the more important the surrounding control architecture becomes.
Permissions matter. Auditability matters. Data boundaries matter. Evaluation matters. Monitoring matters. Escalation matters. Human oversight matters. These cannot simply be controls added after an AI capability has been developed. Increasingly, they need to become part of how the AI system itself is designed.
In healthcare, the most valuable AI may therefore not be the AI that can do the most. It may be the AI that understands precisely what it is allowed to do, under which conditions it should act, and when it should stop and ask for human judgment.

## Beyond Human in the Loop

This also makes me question one of the phrases we frequently use when discussing responsible AI: human in the loop.
The idea is directionally correct, but it can create a false sense of safety. Putting an approval button at the end of an automated process does not necessarily mean a human is meaningfully involved in the decision. If AI has already gathered the information, interpreted the context, selected the relevant evidence, determined the recommended action, and framed the decision, the human may technically approve the outcome while having very little understanding of how that outcome was produced.
What we need is something broader than human approval. We need control architecture.
Control architecture means deciding the boundaries of autonomy before deploying the intelligence. It means understanding which decisions are reversible, which actions require verification, which environments an agent can access, what information it can retrieve, what systems it can modify, when confidence needs to be surfaced, when escalation is mandatory, and where autonomous action simply should not occur.

## The Aviation Lesson

There is an interesting lesson here from aviation.
Modern aircraft are extraordinarily automated. They can navigate, maintain altitude, manage complex systems, detect anomalies, and assist pilots through situations that would have been unimaginable several decades ago. Yet aviation did not become safer simply because aircraft became more intelligent.
Capability evolved alongside operational discipline.
Instrumentation improved. Redundancy was engineered into critical systems. Training evolved. Simulation became routine. Incidents were investigated. Checklists became part of operating culture. Authority and responsibility were clearly defined.
The technology became more powerful because the system surrounding the technology became more sophisticated.
AI will require a similar evolution. We have spent much of the last several years improving the engine. We now need to give equal attention to the cockpit.

## Trust Requires Verification

One of the more interesting proposals in Amodei's essay is the idea of independent evaluation. He argues that frontier AI companies should provide meaningful access to independent evaluators capable of assessing increasingly powerful systems. He also argues for greater coordination between companies and governments around common safety thresholds and eventually some form of international coordination as capabilities increase.
There will be significant debate around how those ideas should work in practice. But underneath them is a principle that I think will become increasingly important for enterprises as well.
Trust requires verification.
AI systems cannot simply be evaluated when they are initially deployed and then treated like static software. Models change. Prompts change. Knowledge changes. Tools change. Permissions change. Data changes. Workflows change. The environment in which an agent operates changes.
Evaluation therefore needs to become continuous.
This is where I think organizations need their own version of pacing the frontier. Most companies cannot determine how quickly OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, or other frontier laboratories advance their models. But every organization controls something equally important.
It controls how quickly it delegates authority to those models.
That distinction gives leaders a much more practical way to think about AI adoption.
AI can first observe a workflow and help humans understand it. It can then recommend actions. It can assist people in performing those actions. Over time, it can execute certain actions with human approval. Eventually, where sufficient evidence, monitoring, and controls exist, it may operate autonomously within carefully defined boundaries.
The important idea is that autonomy should be earned through evidence. A more intelligent model should not automatically receive more authority.

## Two Curves, One Challenge

This changes what it means to be an AI First organization.
AI First cannot simply mean AI fast. It cannot mean placing AI into every workflow or measuring progress by the number of copilots, agents, or automated processes deployed. Being AI First means redesigning work around the capabilities of intelligence while being equally deliberate about where human judgment, accountability, and control remain essential.
Sometimes that will mean removing friction. Sometimes it will mean intentionally creating friction. Sometimes the right architecture will allow an agent to complete an entire workflow. In another situation, the right architecture may deliberately stop the agent before a consequential decision and require human judgment.
The goal is not maximum AI. The goal is maximum value with appropriate control.
I increasingly think leaders need to manage two curves simultaneously. The first is the Capability Curve, representing what AI can do. The second is the Control Curve, representing what we can confidently allow AI to do.
The distance between those two curves is the AI Control Gap.
The objective should not be to flatten the Capability Curve. Innovation will continue, and the potential benefits of increasingly capable AI are too significant to ignore. The challenge is to accelerate the Control Curve.
That means investing in evaluation, observability, AI literacy, security, data architecture, permissions, monitoring, escalation mechanisms, and new forms of operational governance. These investments may initially appear to slow adoption. Over time, I believe they will do exactly the opposite.
Because organizations with stronger control architectures will be able to give AI more responsibility.
That may become one of the most important competitive advantages of the agentic era.
When powerful AI models become broadly available, access to intelligence becomes less differentiating. Most organizations will eventually have access to similar models, agents, reasoning capabilities, and tools. The difference will be what they are prepared to trust those systems to do.
Can an agent access sensitive information? Can it coordinate a workflow? Can it interact with multiple enterprise systems? Can it make a recommendation that influences care? Can it execute an action? Can the organization reconstruct why that action occurred? Can it detect when the system is behaving differently than expected?
Organizations that can answer those questions confidently will be able to delegate more work to AI than organizations that cannot.
And that leads to what I think is the most interesting implication of Amodei's argument.
Control and speed may not ultimately be opposing forces. The organizations that invest most seriously in control may eventually be the organizations capable of moving the fastest.
That is why I do not read We Must Pace the Frontier simply as an argument about slowing down AI development. I read it as a signal that we are approaching a point where the ability to create intelligence may begin exceeding our ability to absorb it.
The next chapter of AI leadership therefore cannot only be about building more capability. It must be about building the organizational capacity to handle that capability.
Because the defining question of the next phase of AI may no longer be how intelligent can these systems become?
It may be how much intelligence are we prepared to responsibly put to work?
