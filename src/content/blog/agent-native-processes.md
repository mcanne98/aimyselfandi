For much of the history of enterprise technology, organizations have designed processes for people and then used software to help those people execute them. We created applications, databases, forms, dashboards and workflows around a fairly simple assumption: somewhere in the process, a person would read the information, understand what it meant, decide what to do next and operate the software.
Agentic AI begins to challenge that assumption.
We are entering a period where intelligence itself can increasingly participate in the execution of work. An AI agent can retrieve information, interpret instructions, use tools, interact with systems, create outputs and potentially initiate actions. But as organizations begin experimenting with these capabilities, they are discovering an interesting constraint. The intelligence may be advancing faster than the processes we are asking it to execute.
The next stage of AI transformation may therefore have less to do with making AI smarter and much more to do with making organizations easier for intelligence to understand.
I think of this as becoming agent native.
An agent native organization does not simply add AI agents to existing workflows. It begins designing its knowledge, processes and systems with the assumption that both people and intelligent agents may participate in the work.
That distinction sounds subtle. Its implications are not.

## From Human Readable to Machine Executable

Consider how organizational information has evolved.
For decades, the primary objective was making information understandable to people. We wrote documents, created websites, developed manuals and designed applications around human interaction.
As enterprise software matured, we made more information machine readable. Databases, structured files, APIs and integration standards allowed systems to exchange information without requiring a person to interpret everything in between.
Generative AI introduced another transition. Information that was never explicitly structured for machines could suddenly become machine understandable. Large language models could interpret documents, conversations, policies and other forms of unstructured information remarkably well.
Agents introduce the next transition.
Human Readable → Machine Readable → Machine Understandable → Machine Executable
The distinction between the last two is particularly important.
Understanding a refund policy is different from determining whether a particular customer qualifies for a refund, gathering the required information, issuing it through the appropriate system, documenting what happened and escalating the transaction when an exception occurs.
Understanding an implementation guide is different from determining which steps apply to a particular customer, collecting missing configuration information, validating dependencies, preparing the environment and identifying decisions requiring human judgment.
Understanding a clinical workflow is different from safely participating in that workflow.
Knowledge alone is not enough. Execution requires structure.

## Agents Are Exposing Something We Have Tolerated for Years

There is a phrase heard in almost every organization: "Ask Sarah. She knows how that works."
Sometimes it is: "Technically that is the process, but we do something different for these customers."
Or: "Don't use that document. There is a newer version somewhere in Teams."
Organizations function despite these inconsistencies because people are remarkably good at navigating ambiguity. We ask questions. We interpret context. We remember exceptions. We learn who really owns a process. We discover which documentation is outdated and eventually develop the institutional knowledge necessary to get the job done.
Agents expose this ambiguity very quickly.
Give an extremely capable AI agent five contradictory procedures, incomplete customer information, unclear permissions and undocumented exceptions, and the problem may not be the intelligence of the agent. The organization may simply be difficult to understand.
This introduces a concept that I believe will become increasingly important as organizations adopt agentic AI: **organizational legibility**.
Organizational legibility is the degree to which an organization can clearly express what it knows, how work should happen, which rules govern that work and where authority resides.
Agents may become one of the greatest tests of organizational legibility we have ever created.
When an agent cannot reliably execute a process, our instinct will often be to improve the model, modify the prompt or introduce another AI tool. Sometimes that will be the correct answer. But increasingly we may discover that the agent is revealing something uncomfortable about the organization itself.
We never actually standardized the process.

## An SOP Is Becoming Something Different

Standard operating procedures have traditionally been written for people. A person reads the document, interprets the instructions and performs the work.
But an interesting change is occurring in the architecture of AI systems. Procedures can increasingly be packaged into reusable instructions and skills that agents can discover and execute.
OpenAI, for example, now describes Skills as reusable workflows that allow ChatGPT to consistently perform recurring work. Skills can contain workflow instructions, examples, resources, schemas and access to tools.
The implications extend far beyond a particular AI platform.
An SOP has traditionally been a knowledge asset. Increasingly, an SOP can become an executable asset.
Instead of simply describing how work should happen, organizations can begin structuring procedures so intelligence can participate in performing that work.
This changes the value of documentation. Documentation has often been treated as something organizations create after the real work has been designed. It captures the process so employees can learn it later.
In an agent native organization, documentation increasingly becomes part of the infrastructure through which work happens. That is a very different role.

## The Internet Is Beginning to Change Too

We can already see early signs of this transition outside internal enterprise processes.
The web was primarily designed around a simple relationship: Human → Browser → Website. Websites therefore became increasingly sophisticated visual experiences designed for human navigation.
But intelligent agents introduce another consumer of information: Agent → Information.
An agent does not necessarily need elaborate navigation, animations, menus or visual formatting. It needs access to the underlying information in a form that can be efficiently understood.
This is one reason formats such as Markdown and emerging conventions for publishing AI accessible web content are becoming interesting. They strip away much of the presentation layer and expose the underlying information more directly.
Markdown itself is not the important development. The important development is that organizations are beginning to recognize that humans may no longer be the only audience consuming their digital resources.
The same principle will increasingly apply inside organizations. Policies, product documentation, implementation procedures, support playbooks, operational workflows and training material will increasingly have two audiences: the people who need to understand them and the intelligent systems that may need to act upon them.

## From Knowledge to Capability to Authority

This transition becomes easier to understand if we separate three concepts that are often combined in discussions about enterprise AI.
The first is knowledge. What does the organization know? Generative AI and retrieval systems have made enormous progress here. Organizations are connecting models to documents, databases, conversations and enterprise knowledge so AI can answer questions with organizational context.
The second is capability. What does the organization know how to do? This is where structured procedures, agent skills and workflows become important. Instead of merely retrieving information about a process, an agent can increasingly understand the sequence required to perform it.
But there is a third layer that becomes considerably more important once agents begin acting.
Authority. What is the agent actually allowed to do?
An agent may understand how an invoice should be processed without having authority to approve payment. It may understand how a customer environment should be configured without having permission to modify it. It may identify an anomaly in healthcare data without having authority to change the clinical record.
Knowledge, capability and authority are different things. Agent native process design must distinguish between them.
This is why emerging agent architectures increasingly include concepts such as tools, permissions, authorization and identity. Once intelligence moves from generating information to performing actions, organizations must establish boundaries around what that intelligence can access, change, approve or initiate.
At that point, AI governance stops being an abstract policy discussion. It becomes part of process design.

## The Agent Native Process Contract

This raises a practical question. What actually makes a process agent native?
I think organizations can begin with a simple mental model. Every repeatable process should increasingly be capable of expressing seven things clearly:
**Context → Inputs → Rules → Authority → Actions → Exceptions → Outcomes**
Think of this as an Agent Native Process Contract.
Context establishes what must be understood before the process begins. Inputs define the information required to perform the work. Rules establish the policies, constraints and decision logic governing the process. Authority defines what the agent is permitted to access, decide or change. Actions define which tools and systems can be used to execute the work. Exceptions define when the normal process should stop and human judgment or another workflow should take over. Outcomes define what successful completion actually means.
This framework is useful even if an organization has no intention of automating the process today.
Take an existing SOP and try to answer those seven questions. If the answers are unclear, scattered across multiple systems or dependent on someone saying "you just need to know," the organization has discovered something important. It has found organizational ambiguity.
The AI agent did not create that ambiguity. It simply made it visible.

## Healthcare Shows Why Agent Native Cannot Mean Agent Autonomous

Healthcare provides a particularly useful stress test for this idea because many workflows combine structured processes with situations where context, judgment and accountability matter enormously.
Consider a clinical documentation workflow. An agent might gather relevant information, organize documentation, identify missing fields and prepare a draft for review. That does not necessarily mean the agent should independently determine what becomes part of the clinical record.
Consider interoperability troubleshooting. An agent might collect identifiers, inspect transaction histories, compare expected and actual data, recognize known failure patterns and prepare recommended remediation. Certain changes, however, may require authorization or specialist review.
Or consider prior authorization. An intelligent system could assemble relevant documentation, validate whether required information is present, identify the applicable criteria and prepare a submission. The boundary between administrative execution and clinical judgment still matters.
These examples reveal an important distinction. Agent native does not mean agent autonomous.
The objective is not to redesign every process until people disappear. The objective is to make the division between machine execution and human judgment intentional.
Some processes may eventually become highly autonomous. Others may operate through agents preparing work for human approval. Some may allow agents to execute actions within defined limits and escalate exceptions. And some decisions should remain fundamentally human.
The architecture of work therefore begins to shift from simply asking who performs the task to determining where judgment belongs.

## Stop Adding Agents to Broken Processes

This may be one of the most important lessons for organizations beginning their agentic AI journey.
The temptation will be to take an existing process and add an agent. Customer support process plus agent. Implementation process plus agent. Finance process plus agent. Clinical workflow plus agent.
That approach resembles many earlier digital transformation programs where organizations digitized inefficient processes without reconsidering why those processes existed in the first place.
Agentic AI gives us an opportunity to do something different.
Instead of asking: Where can we add an agent?
Organizations should increasingly ask: If this process were designed today knowing that people and intelligent agents could work together, how would we design it differently?
That question changes the conversation. Maybe information would be structured differently. Maybe approvals would be clearer. Maybe exceptions would be explicitly documented. Maybe APIs would expose capabilities currently buried inside user interfaces. Maybe five procedural documents would become one governed source of truth. Maybe the process itself would disappear.
This is why becoming agent native is not primarily an AI implementation exercise. It is process redesign.

## The New Value of Standardization

For years, standardization has sometimes been viewed as the enemy of flexibility. Organizations wanted employees to use judgment, adapt to customers and respond to unusual situations. That remains important.
But agentic AI creates a different reason to standardize. Standardization creates a reliable foundation from which intelligence can operate.
The goal is not to eliminate variation. It is to distinguish between intentional variation and accidental variation.
If ten employees perform the same task differently because each situation genuinely requires different judgment, that may be appropriate. If they perform it differently because the process has never been clearly defined, that is organizational debt.
Agents will expose that debt.
Organizations that reduce it will be able to automate more reliably, onboard people faster, measure outcomes more consistently and improve processes more systematically. Agent readiness may therefore become an unexpected catalyst for operational excellence.

## Leadership's Next Delegation Problem

There is an even larger implication. Organizations have spent centuries learning how to delegate authority to people.
We create roles. We define responsibilities. We train employees. We give them credentials. We establish approval limits. We create escalation paths. We audit decisions. Managers determine where employees can exercise judgment and where additional approval is required.
These systems are so familiar that we rarely think about them as technological architecture. But they are. They are the architecture through which organizations distribute authority.
Agentic AI introduces a new participant into that architecture.
For the first time, organizations will increasingly need to decide not only what intelligence can know, but what intelligence can do.
Can the agent draft? Can it recommend? Can it approve? Can it modify? Can it communicate externally? Can it spend money? Can it change a customer environment? Can it initiate another workflow? Can it make a decision without human review?
These are not simply technical questions. They are leadership questions.
The deepest challenge of becoming agent native may therefore have very little to do with AI itself.
It requires organizations to become more explicit about how they operate. What do we know? How does work actually happen? Where are the rules? Where are the exceptions? Where does judgment belong? And what authority are we prepared to delegate?
The organizations best prepared for the agentic era may not ultimately be those with the largest number of agents or the most sophisticated models. They may be the organizations that have done the harder work of making themselves understandable: turning institutional knowledge into accessible knowledge, turning repeatable procedures into executable capabilities and defining clear boundaries around authority.
Because before intelligence can reliably execute the work of an organization, the organization first has to be capable of explaining how its work should be done.
