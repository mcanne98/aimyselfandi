In the first article of this series, I argued that great AI starts long before the prompt. The biggest improvements don't come from learning clever prompting techniques. They come from designing a better thinking workflow.

In the second article, we explored why every successful AI conversation begins with clearly defined goals. Before asking AI to produce anything, we need to define what success actually looks like. Great prompts answer questions. Great goals define success.

But goals alone are not enough.

You can define success perfectly and still receive an average response from AI. Not because the model lacks intelligence, but because it lacks the one thing that gives intelligence direction: context.

I've become increasingly convinced that context is the most valuable asset in modern AI. As models continue to improve, the differences between them are becoming smaller. Every new release reasons better, writes better, codes better, and understands language better than the one before it. Those improvements are important, but they are also becoming expected.

What increasingly separates exceptional AI outcomes from average ones is not the model itself. It's the information the model has available before it begins reasoning.

That's why I believe we are entering a new era where context, not intelligence, becomes the real competitive advantage.

Think about the people on your own team. Imagine hiring someone with exceptional analytical skills, outstanding communication abilities, and years of experience solving complex problems. On their first day, would you ask them to present your three year strategy to the executive team? Would you expect them to redesign your customer onboarding process or advise your largest client?

Probably not.

Their intelligence isn't the issue.

Their lack of context is.

They don't yet understand your customers, your products, your priorities, your culture, your constraints, or the decisions that brought you to where you are today. Until they build that understanding, even the smartest employee will produce work that feels generic.

AI operates exactly the same way.

When we ask AI to create a presentation, write a proposal, or analyze a problem without providing meaningful context, we are asking it to make decisions without understanding the environment in which those decisions will be used. The result is often technically correct, but strategically incomplete.

I've found that one of the biggest misconceptions about AI is the belief that intelligence alone is enough. It isn't.

Without context, AI becomes incredibly educated and surprisingly uninformed.

That sentence has become a useful reminder for me. Large language models have been trained on an extraordinary amount of public information. They understand business frameworks, programming languages, healthcare terminology, financial concepts, psychology, marketing, and countless other domains. Yet they know almost nothing about your organization unless you deliberately teach them.

They don't know why one customer is strategically important while another is not.

They don't know why a particular architectural decision was made eighteen months ago.

They don't know which implementation challenges your Professional Services team encounters repeatedly.

They don't know your writing style, your leadership philosophy, or the decisions your executive team has already rejected.

Without that information, AI fills the gaps using probability. Sometimes it guesses correctly. Sometimes it doesn't. Either way, it is reasoning with an incomplete picture.

I've started referring to this difference as the Context Gap.

The Context Gap is the distance between what you know and what AI knows.

Every time AI produces an answer that feels generic, every time it suggests something you've already tried, or every time it ignores an important business constraint, you are often seeing the Context Gap in action.

Closing that gap is one of the highest leverage activities any leader can invest in.

The obvious question is, what actually counts as context?

Many people assume context is simply uploading a document before asking a question. While that certainly helps, organizational context is much broader than a single file.

Context includes meeting notes, customer conversations, architecture decisions, implementation history, product documentation, CRM records, project plans, support cases, technical standards, strategic priorities, governance policies, previous presentations, stakeholder feedback, and lessons learned from earlier projects.

It also includes something less tangible: the way you think.

Over the past year, I've invested significant time helping AI understand how I approach leadership, strategy, technical architecture, healthcare, and communication. The objective wasn't to teach AI facts. It was to teach it how I make decisions.

That investment changes almost every conversation I have.

When I'm preparing an executive presentation, AI already understands the audience I typically write for. It understands the level of technical depth I prefer. It understands the writing style I've developed through this newsletter. It understands previous strategies we've discussed, recurring themes, and the frameworks I've created.

The conversation no longer starts from zero.

It starts from accumulated knowledge.

That's the power of context.

This is also why I believe organizations need to stop thinking about AI as isolated conversations and start thinking about it as a continuously evolving knowledge system.

Every customer meeting generates new context.

Every implementation produces new lessons.

Every architecture review captures valuable decisions.

Every support case reveals patterns.

Every executive discussion creates institutional knowledge.

The question is whether that knowledge disappears into emails, chat messages, and meeting recordings, or whether it becomes reusable context that improves every future AI interaction.

This is where I've found Claude to be particularly effective.

One of the capabilities I value most is Claude Projects combined with Claude Skills.

Instead of treating every conversation as independent, I organize my work into dedicated projects. Each project contains the documentation, reference material, standards, writing style, architecture, and background information relevant to that specific domain.

For example, I maintain separate projects for my AI newsletter, Technical Services strategy, Professional Services AI initiatives, executive presentations, and Knowledge Protocol Engineering work. Each project contains curated context that Claude can reference throughout our conversations.

On top of that, I use Claude Skills to standardize repeatable workflows.

Rather than rewriting the same instructions every time, I create reusable skills such as /goals, /context, /assumptions, /plan, /draft, /review, and /iterate. Each skill contains detailed guidance about how Claude should approach that stage of the workflow.

When I invoke /review, Claude already understands that I want it to evaluate alignment, evidence, decision quality, stakeholder impact, blind spots, and strategic risk rather than simply proofreading grammar.

When I invoke /draft, it understands the writing style, audience, structure, and objectives I've previously defined.

These skills dramatically reduce repetition while increasing consistency.

More importantly, they transform AI from a reactive assistant into a repeatable workflow.

This is where I believe many organizations are heading.

Rather than relying on individual prompt engineering, they'll build libraries of reusable skills, organizational knowledge, and standardized workflows that every employee can leverage.

The intelligence becomes shared.

The context becomes institutional.

The capability becomes scalable.

Of course, building context doesn't happen automatically.

Organizations accumulate what I call Context Debt.

Context Debt exists whenever valuable organizational knowledge cannot be accessed by either people or AI.

It appears when important decisions live only inside someone's memory. It appears when project lessons are never documented. It appears when customer knowledge is scattered across email, CRM notes, shared drives, and meeting recordings without any meaningful connection between them.

Most organizations don't suffer from a lack of knowledge.

They suffer from knowledge that cannot be found, connected, or reused.

AI simply exposes that weakness.

The organizations that gain the greatest advantage from AI over the next decade won't necessarily be the ones with access to the newest models. They'll be the ones that systematically capture, organize, maintain, and reuse context across every part of the business.

As foundation models continue to converge, intelligence becomes increasingly available to everyone.

Context does not.

Context is built through discipline. It grows through every customer interaction, every project, every decision, and every lesson learned. Unlike models, which improve for everyone simultaneously, your organizational context is unique. It reflects your experience, your customers, your people, and your way of working.

That makes it incredibly difficult to copy.

I believe that's where the next generation of competitive advantage will come from.

Not simply asking AI better questions.

Building organizations that continuously provide AI with better context.
