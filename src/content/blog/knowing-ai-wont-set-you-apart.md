The next competitive advantage belongs to those who can turn AI knowledge into meaningful outcomes.

For the past few years, learning artificial intelligence has been one of the most valuable investments professionals could make in their careers. Those who embraced the technology early, experimented with prompting, explored automation, and incorporated AI into their daily routines quickly distinguished themselves from their peers. They became the people others turned to for guidance, the ones introducing new possibilities, and often the individuals recognized for bringing innovation into their organizations.

But the landscape is changing.

As AI becomes increasingly accessible, the very skills that once made someone stand out are gradually becoming expected. Organizations are investing in AI training, integrating intelligent capabilities into enterprise applications, and encouraging employees to incorporate AI into their responsibilities. What was once considered innovative is steadily becoming part of the normal working environment.

We have seen this transition before. There was a time when knowing how to use Microsoft Excel was a meaningful professional advantage. Employees who could build sophisticated spreadsheets, analyze information, and automate calculations were highly valued. Eventually, spreadsheet proficiency became a standard workplace expectation. The people who continued to distinguish themselves were not necessarily those who knew the most formulas. They were the ones who understood how to use those capabilities to solve important business problems, improve financial decisions, and uncover opportunities that others had overlooked.

AI is approaching a similar inflection point, although its implications are considerably more profound. Unlike previous productivity technologies, AI is beginning to participate in activities that have traditionally depended on human knowledge, reasoning, and judgment. It can analyze information, generate software, synthesize research, and increasingly execute complex workflows.

This changes the nature of professional differentiation.

Knowing how to use AI is becoming the foundation. Knowing how to create value with AI is becoming the advantage.

## The AI Proficiency Paradox

Organizations have spent considerable time and resources building AI proficiency across their workforce. Training programs have been introduced, communities of practice established, and employees encouraged to experiment with increasingly capable tools. These investments have been necessary because technological transformation cannot happen when people lack the confidence or skills to engage with the technology.

However, these efforts introduce an interesting paradox. The more successful organizations become at developing AI proficiency, the less distinctive basic proficiency becomes.

Consider an employee who uses AI to summarize meetings, prepare presentations, draft communications, or analyze documents. These capabilities can significantly improve personal productivity. Tasks that previously required hours can sometimes be completed in minutes, allowing individuals to focus their attention elsewhere.

But what happens when everyone has access to the same capabilities?

The productivity improvement remains valuable, but it no longer necessarily distinguishes one employee from another. It becomes part of the organization's normal operating environment, much like email, spreadsheets, and digital collaboration tools became essential components of modern work.

This does not mean AI proficiency loses its importance. Quite the opposite. It becomes increasingly essential. The distinction is that proficiency alone is no longer sufficient to create a sustained professional advantage.

We need to separate the ability to operate a technology from the ability to use that technology to create meaningful change.

Someone can become exceptionally proficient at prompting without ever questioning whether the process they are accelerating should continue to exist. They can generate better reports without considering whether those reports are still necessary. They can automate individual tasks while leaving the broader workflow fragmented and inefficient.

In other words, they can become very good at using AI without fundamentally changing the value they contribute.

The real opportunity begins when we stop measuring what AI helps us produce and start examining what AI allows us to change.

## The Three Levels of AI Value Creation

I believe we need a more useful way of thinking about AI proficiency, one that connects technological capability with the outcomes individuals and organizations create.

A practical framework is to distinguish between three approaches to AI value creation: AI Users, AI Integrators, and AI Architects.

These are not job titles, levels of seniority, or permanent classifications. They represent different ways people apply AI to their work. An individual may operate at all three levels depending on the problem they are solving.

### AI Users: Improving How We Execute

At the first level, individuals use AI to perform existing responsibilities more efficiently. They understand how to interact with AI systems, provide appropriate context, evaluate outputs, and incorporate the results into their daily activities.

Consider a healthcare implementation consultant responsible for onboarding a new customer. Traditionally, the consultant participates in discovery meetings, documents requirements, prepares project updates, and coordinates activities across multiple teams. By incorporating AI, the consultant can summarize conversations, organize requirements, draft documentation, and identify outstanding actions much faster.

The improvement can be substantial. The consultant spends less time on administrative activities and has more capacity to engage with customers and address complex implementation challenges.

But the underlying implementation process remains largely unchanged. Meetings still generate documentation, documentation still moves between teams, and information may still need to be manually transferred across systems.

The individual is working faster, but the organization is essentially operating the same way.

This is where many professionals begin their AI journey, and it remains an important foundation. However, it should not be confused with the full potential of the technology.

### AI Integrators: Improving How Work Flows

At the second level, individuals begin incorporating AI into the processes through which work gets completed.

Rather than focusing exclusively on personal productivity, they examine how information moves between people, systems, and activities. They identify repetitive interactions, unnecessary handoffs, disconnected information, and opportunities to introduce intelligent capabilities into existing workflows.

Returning to our healthcare implementation example, imagine that customer discovery conversations are automatically transformed into structured requirements. Those requirements are compared against product capabilities, configuration dependencies are identified, and recommendations are generated for implementation specialists to validate.

Information no longer needs to be repeatedly interpreted and transferred between disconnected documents. Instead, AI becomes part of the workflow, connecting activities that previously depended on manual coordination.

The consultant is no longer simply using AI to complete individual tasks faster. They are helping establish a more efficient way for the entire team to operate.

The distinction is important because the value begins to extend beyond the individual. Improvements become repeatable, knowledge becomes more accessible, and the organization starts benefiting from capabilities that can be shared across teams.

At this level, AI is no longer just a personal productivity tool. It becomes an operational capability.

### AI Architects: Redesigning How Value Is Created

The third level introduces a fundamentally different question.

Instead of asking how AI can improve an existing process, AI Architects ask whether that process should continue to exist in its current form.

Consider the traditional approach to implementing enterprise healthcare software. Customer requirements are collected, translated into configuration decisions, documented, reviewed, implemented, tested, and eventually handed over to operational teams.

Each stage has evolved over time to address a particular business need. Documentation provides traceability, reviews establish accountability, and handoffs ensure that responsibilities move between teams.

But many of these activities also exist because information has historically been difficult to transfer, validate, and maintain across organizational boundaries.

What happens when AI can maintain structured knowledge about customer requirements, product capabilities, configuration rules, and implementation decisions throughout the entire lifecycle?

We can begin imagining an implementation environment where requirements continuously inform configuration recommendations, dependencies are evaluated as decisions are made, validation occurs throughout the process, and documentation evolves alongside the implementation itself.

Human expertise remains essential. Specialists still exercise judgment, validate decisions, manage exceptions, and remain accountable for customer outcomes. But the process no longer needs to be organized around every manual activity that existed before AI.

The objective is not simply to make implementation faster. It is to reconsider how implementation should work when information, intelligence, and execution can be more closely connected.

This is the difference between optimizing a process and redesigning an operating model.

AI Users improve execution. AI Integrators improve workflows. AI Architects reconsider how value is created.

And importantly, becoming an AI Architect does not require becoming a software engineer. It requires understanding a business problem deeply enough to recognize how technology could change the way it is solved.

## The Next Career Advantage Is Problem Selection

For much of our professional lives, expertise has been associated with our ability to perform particular activities exceptionally well. We develop specialized knowledge, become proficient with tools, and build reputations around the quality and reliability of our execution.

AI is beginning to challenge this relationship.

As intelligent systems become more capable of generating content, analyzing information, developing software, and executing increasingly complex activities, the ability to perform individual tasks may become less scarce.

However, the ability to recognize which problems deserve attention becomes more valuable.

Imagine two employees working within the same organization. The first has invested considerable time mastering AI tools. They understand advanced prompting techniques, can generate sophisticated reports, and have automated several activities within their personal workflow.

The second has a more modest understanding of AI but recognizes that customer onboarding requires information to be entered into multiple systems, involves repeated validation, and creates unnecessary delays between teams.

Rather than simply automating their portion of the process, the second employee begins asking why the information must be collected multiple times, whether existing systems could share a common understanding of customer requirements, and how AI might eliminate unnecessary coordination.

They engage colleagues, validate assumptions, and help introduce a redesigned workflow that improves onboarding outcomes for the entire organization.

Both employees have developed valuable capabilities. But the second has translated technological opportunity into broader business impact.

This illustrates an important shift in how professional value may increasingly be created.

The ability to identify the right problem can become more valuable than the ability to execute a solution.

This does not diminish the importance of technical expertise. Building reliable, secure, and effective AI systems requires substantial engineering knowledge. But it highlights why domain expertise, curiosity, and business judgment remain critical.

AI can help us explore solutions. It cannot independently determine which organizational outcomes deserve priority, which tradeoffs are acceptable, or which changes will create sustainable value.

Those decisions require an understanding of context that extends beyond the technology itself.

## From Personal Productivity to Organizational Intelligence

There is another dimension to this transformation that I believe organizations are only beginning to appreciate.

Most AI adoption initiatives start with individuals. Employees experiment with prompts, develop useful techniques, discover ways to improve their work, and gradually become more proficient.

The challenge is that much of this knowledge remains personal.

An employee may spend several hours refining a prompt that analyzes customer requirements and identifies implementation risks. The result may be exceptionally effective, but if that knowledge remains inside an individual conversation or personal workspace, its organizational value is limited.

The employee has become more capable. The organization has not necessarily developed a lasting capability.

Now consider what happens when that same expertise is captured as a reusable AI skill, supported by structured instructions, relevant knowledge, validation rules, and appropriate governance.

Other employees can apply the same capability. AI agents can invoke it within broader workflows. The process can be evaluated, refined, and improved as new information becomes available.

Knowledge begins to accumulate rather than being repeatedly recreated.

This connects directly to the emerging importance of Agent Native workflows, where organizational knowledge is designed not only for human consumption but also for intelligent systems capable of interpreting and acting upon it.

A successful AI interaction should not always end with a useful answer. In many cases, it should create an opportunity to capture a reusable capability.

Consider a healthcare organization where a clinical specialist develops an effective AI assisted approach for identifying gaps in discharge documentation. Initially, this capability improves the specialist's personal productivity.

But imagine the organization captures the underlying knowledge, validates the clinical rules, establishes appropriate privacy and security controls, and integrates the capability into a governed workflow with clinical oversight.

The benefit can now extend beyond one individual to multiple care teams, while maintaining the accountability required in a clinical environment.

This introduces a distinction that should influence how organizations approach AI adoption.

Personal AI proficiency improves what an individual can accomplish. Organizational AI capability improves what everyone can accomplish.

The professionals who recognize this difference will increasingly contribute value beyond the boundaries of their immediate responsibilities.

## Are We Measuring the Wrong Things?

As organizations accelerate their AI adoption initiatives, leaders are naturally looking for ways to measure progress.

Training completion rates, active users, licenses deployed, prompts submitted, and participation in AI communities provide useful indicators of engagement. They help organizations understand whether employees are becoming comfortable with the technology and whether investments in enablement are reaching the workforce.

But these measurements have an important limitation.

They tell us whether people are using AI. They do not necessarily tell us whether AI is improving the organization.

A company could achieve impressive adoption rates while continuing to operate with fragmented processes, duplicated information, unnecessary handoffs, and significant operational inefficiencies.

Employees might become exceptionally efficient at completing activities that should no longer require manual execution.

This is why I believe we need to distinguish between AI activity and AI impact.

Consider a healthcare technology organization reporting that 85% of its implementation consultants use AI every week. This may represent meaningful progress in adoption, but it tells us relatively little about the value being created.

A more revealing set of questions would examine whether implementation cycle times have improved, whether configuration accuracy has increased, whether customers are reaching value sooner, and whether operational effort has decreased without compromising quality.

These outcomes connect AI investments to business performance.

They also encourage a different kind of employee contribution.

When organizations celebrate the number of AI tools being used, employees naturally focus on demonstrating adoption. When organizations recognize measurable improvements in customer outcomes, operational performance, and organizational capability, employees have stronger incentives to identify meaningful opportunities for change.

This raises a question every leader should consider.

If every employee in your organization became AI proficient tomorrow, which business outcomes would actually improve?

If the answer is unclear, the organization may have a well developed AI adoption strategy without an equally developed strategy for creating value.

## The Human Capabilities That Become More Important

One of the more interesting consequences of AI becoming widely accessible is that several distinctly human capabilities may become more valuable, not less.

For years, professional development has often emphasized acquiring specialized knowledge and mastering the tools required to perform particular responsibilities. These capabilities remain important, but AI is changing how expertise is accessed and applied.

When information becomes easier to retrieve, the ability to interpret its relevance becomes more important. When solutions become easier to generate, the ability to evaluate their consequences becomes more valuable. And when execution becomes increasingly automated, the ability to determine what should be executed becomes essential.

Judgment sits at the center of this transition.

AI systems can generate recommendations, identify patterns, and evaluate alternatives, but their outputs must still be considered within the context of business priorities, operational constraints, ethical responsibilities, and potential consequences.

In healthcare, this distinction becomes especially important. An AI system may help identify patterns in clinical documentation or highlight potential gaps in care coordination. But determining whether a recommendation is appropriate requires an understanding of clinical context, patient needs, regulatory obligations, and the consequences of acting on incomplete or inaccurate information.

Systems thinking becomes equally important.

Many organizational problems are not caused by a lack of individual productivity. They emerge from how processes, information, technologies, and responsibilities interact. Improving one activity may create limited value if the broader system remains inefficient.

Professionals who understand these relationships are better positioned to identify opportunities that extend beyond their immediate responsibilities.

The ability to frame problems also becomes increasingly valuable. AI can help generate numerous possible solutions, but poorly defined problems often produce impressive outputs that fail to address the underlying business need.

Finally, the ability to influence change becomes essential. Redesigning work is not simply a technical exercise. It requires building trust, engaging stakeholders, addressing uncertainty, and helping people develop confidence in new ways of operating.

Technology can make a new operating model possible. People determine whether that model becomes reality.

As AI makes execution more accessible, judgment, problem framing, systems thinking, and change leadership become increasingly important sources of professional value.

## We May Need to Redefine What It Means to Be a High Performer

This brings us to what I believe is one of the most significant leadership implications of the AI transformation.

For decades, organizations have developed performance management systems around the contributions individuals make within established roles and processes. We recognize employees who consistently deliver high quality work, exceed productivity expectations, demonstrate deep expertise, and take on additional responsibilities.

These remain important characteristics of strong performance.

But AI introduces a new dimension that traditional performance systems may not adequately recognize.

Consider an employee who consistently completes ten complex activities each week with exceptional accuracy and reliability. Their performance is visible, measurable, and relatively easy to evaluate.

Now consider another employee who identifies an opportunity to redesign the process so that those same ten activities can be completed through an intelligent workflow, requiring human involvement primarily for exceptions and critical decisions.

The second employee may initially appear less productive because they spend time experimenting, collaborating, challenging assumptions, and developing an alternative approach.

Yet their contribution could ultimately improve the performance of an entire team.

Which employee should be considered the higher performer?

The answer depends on the quality, sustainability, and consequences of the outcomes created. Reliable execution still matters, particularly in environments where accuracy and accountability are critical. But organizations need to become better at recognizing contributions that improve the system rather than simply increase the volume of work completed within it.

This represents a meaningful change in how we think about talent.

Historically, high performers have often been distinguished by their ability to accomplish more than others.

In an AI enabled organization, some of the most valuable employees may be those who enable everyone else to accomplish more.

Their contribution is multiplied through the processes they improve, the knowledge they make reusable, and the capabilities they help establish.

This also challenges how leaders allocate time and resources.

If employees are evaluated exclusively on immediate output, they have limited incentives to invest in redesigning how work gets done. Experimentation may appear to compete with productivity, even when it creates the conditions for significant future improvements.

Leaders who want meaningful AI transformation must therefore create space for employees to question existing processes, test alternatives, and collaborate across organizational boundaries.

They must also recognize that not everyone needs to become an AI Architect.

Organizations will continue to depend on exceptional practitioners, domain specialists, relationship builders, and operational experts. The objective is not to turn every employee into a transformation leader. It is to create an environment where individuals can recognize opportunities to improve how work gets done and contribute those ideas without being constrained by their position or technical background.

An employee closest to a process may understand its limitations better than anyone else. With the right support, that understanding can become the foundation for meaningful innovation.

The responsibility of leadership is to make that contribution possible and ensure that improvements can be translated into lasting organizational capabilities.

## Learning AI Is the Beginning, Not the Destination

We are entering a period where the relationship between knowledge, execution, and professional value is being reconsidered.

For much of our careers, we have been encouraged to develop expertise in performing our responsibilities exceptionally well. We invest in education, acquire certifications, master tools, and build experience that allows us to become increasingly effective within our chosen professions.

AI does not eliminate the value of that expertise. It changes how we can apply it.

The opportunity is no longer limited to becoming faster or more efficient at completing the work we already know.

It extends to questioning why the work exists, how it should be organized, and whether entirely different approaches could create better outcomes.

This is where I believe the next generation of professional differentiation will emerge.

The individuals who stand out will not necessarily be those who have completed the most AI courses, mastered the largest number of tools, or developed the most sophisticated prompts.

They will be the people who combine AI proficiency with business understanding, sound judgment, curiosity, and the ability to translate opportunities into meaningful improvements.

They will recognize that their greatest contribution may not come from what they personally produce, but from what they make possible for others.

For leaders, this requires an equally important shift. We must move beyond encouraging employees to use AI and begin creating environments where they can challenge how work is performed, contribute to process redesign, and develop reusable capabilities that strengthen the organization.

We must also reconsider how we define and recognize exceptional performance.

Because the employee who becomes twice as productive creates value. But the employee who helps an entire organization operate differently may create something far more significant.

Learning AI remains essential. It opens doors, expands possibilities, and provides the foundation for participating in a rapidly changing workplace.

But it is no longer the destination.

The future will not necessarily reward those who know the most about AI. It will reward those who know what to do with it.
