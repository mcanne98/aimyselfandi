*Why the leadership conversation must shift from job security to career adaptability*

For the past few years, leaders have been repeating a familiar message about artificial intelligence: AI will not replace people, but people who use AI will replace those who do not. It is a compelling statement that encourages employees to embrace technology, challenges resistance to change, and reinforces the importance of developing new skills. At a time when organizations were introducing generative AI into everyday work, it helped create a sense of urgency around adoption.

But I believe we have reached a point where this message is no longer sufficient. As AI becomes capable of reasoning, executing complex tasks, coordinating workflows, and operating with increasing independence, the conversation about its impact on employment needs to evolve. We cannot continue reassuring employees that their jobs are safe simply because they learn how to use AI. Nor should we suggest that anyone who struggles to adapt will inevitably be replaced by someone who does.

Both perspectives oversimplify a much more significant transformation. The reality is that AI will change the nature of work. Some responsibilities will disappear, others will become more valuable, and entirely new roles will emerge. Certain positions may require fewer people, while new opportunities will develop in areas we are only beginning to understand. For leaders, this creates a responsibility that goes well beyond encouraging AI adoption.

**Our responsibility is not to promise that jobs will remain unchanged. It is to help people remain relevant as the work around them changes.**

And that requires a fundamentally different leadership conversation.

## We Are Asking the Wrong Question About Jobs

Much of the public discussion about AI and employment focuses on a single question: Will AI replace my job? It is an understandable concern. Employment provides financial security, professional identity, personal confidence, and a sense of purpose. When technology begins performing activities that once required years of experience, people naturally question what that means for their future.

But the question assumes that jobs are fixed structures that either survive technological change or disappear entirely. In reality, a job is a collection of activities, responsibilities, decisions, relationships, and outcomes. These elements do not all evolve at the same pace.

Consider the role of a technical implementation specialist in a healthcare technology organization. Historically, much of the work might involve gathering customer requirements, preparing configuration files, validating data, documenting decisions, troubleshooting integration issues, and coordinating with different teams. AI is increasingly capable of assisting with many of these activities, and over time, intelligent agents may execute portions of the work with limited human intervention.

Does that mean the implementation specialist becomes unnecessary? Not necessarily. The specialist still brings an understanding of customer environments, operational constraints, complex dependencies, and the consequences of implementation decisions. These capabilities remain important, particularly when technology must operate reliably in environments as complex as healthcare. However, the distribution of work changes. The value of the role may shift away from manually producing configurations and documentation toward designing implementation approaches, validating intelligent systems, resolving complex exceptions, and ensuring successful customer outcomes.

The job title may remain the same, but the capabilities required to perform the role successfully could be substantially different. We should also recognize the economic implications. If AI allows an organization to complete significantly more work with the same resources, the organization may eventually reconsider how many people are needed for certain activities.

**The real question is not simply whether AI will replace a job. It is how much of the work that defines that job will continue to require human effort, and what new responsibilities will take its place.**

That is a more difficult conversation, but it is also a more useful one.

## Experience Is Valuable, but Its Value Is Changing

For decades, professional experience has been one of the most reliable sources of career advantage. The longer someone worked in a particular field, the more knowledge they accumulated. They learned how systems operated, how problems were solved, how decisions were made, and which approaches produced the best results. This experience created expertise that was difficult to replicate.

Artificial intelligence is changing the economics of that knowledge. Information that previously required years of exposure can now be accessed, synthesized, and explained in seconds. Technical documentation can be interpreted by intelligent systems, complex code can be generated and reviewed, and patterns across large volumes of information can be identified without requiring someone to manually examine every record.

This does not make experience irrelevant. It changes where experience creates the greatest value. Knowing how to perform a task remains useful, but understanding why the task matters, when it should be performed, and whether the outcome is appropriate becomes increasingly important.

Consider a software developer who has spent years mastering a particular programming language. As AI becomes more capable of generating code, the developer's competitive advantage may increasingly come from understanding system architecture, identifying security risks, evaluating design decisions, and ensuring that software meets business requirements. The knowledge accumulated over years remains valuable because it supports judgment, but relying exclusively on the ability to produce code may become a less durable advantage.

The same principle applies to project managers, analysts, customer support professionals, and many other roles. The skills that made someone successful yesterday may not be sufficient to sustain that success tomorrow. This is not a criticism of experience. It is a recognition that the relationship between knowledge and professional value is changing.

**Experience tells us what has worked before. Adaptability determines whether we can remain effective when the conditions change.**

For employees, this means professional development can no longer be treated as something that happens occasionally through formal training or career transitions. Learning must become part of how we work. For leaders, it means recognizing that even our most experienced and successful employees may need support in developing capabilities that did not exist when they began their careers.

## From Job Security to Career Adaptability

I believe we need to introduce a different way of thinking about career resilience in the age of AI. For much of the modern workplace, organizations and employees have operated around the concept of job security. Employees developed expertise, demonstrated loyalty, delivered results, and expected that strong performance would provide some degree of employment stability. While this relationship has never guaranteed permanent employment, it has influenced how people think about their careers.

AI challenges that assumption because strong performance in a role today does not necessarily mean the role will remain unchanged tomorrow. This creates an important distinction between three concepts: job security, skill security, and career adaptability.

Job security focuses on preserving the position someone currently holds. It assumes that continuity in the role is the primary source of professional stability. Skill security focuses on maintaining capabilities that remain valuable in the labour market. It recognizes that particular skills can become more or less relevant as technology and business requirements evolve. Career adaptability goes further. It is the ability to continuously learn, apply existing experience in new contexts, develop emerging capabilities, and move toward opportunities as the nature of work changes.

Of the three, I believe career adaptability offers the most durable foundation for professional resilience. Someone may become highly proficient with today's AI tools, but those tools will continue to evolve. Interfaces will change, models will improve, and activities that currently require sophisticated prompting may eventually become automated capabilities embedded directly into business applications.

If we define AI readiness simply as knowing how to use a particular tool, we risk preparing people for a moment in time rather than a changing future. The more valuable capability is learning how to identify opportunities, understand new technologies, evaluate their limitations, and apply them to meaningful business problems.

This requires curiosity, critical thinking, domain knowledge, judgment, and a willingness to challenge familiar ways of working. It also requires an environment where employees can experiment, make mistakes, and develop without feeling that every attempt to learn is a test of their professional worth.

Career adaptability cannot be built through fear alone. It requires confidence that learning is valued, opportunities are accessible, and people have a meaningful role in shaping the future of their work.

## Learning AI Is Not the Same as Preparing for AI

There is an important distinction between becoming proficient with artificial intelligence and preparing for the organizational changes it will create. Today, many companies measure AI readiness through training participation, tool adoption, and the number of employees actively using generative AI. These are reasonable starting points, but an employee who learns how to write effective prompts has not necessarily developed the capabilities required to operate in an organization where intelligent agents perform significant portions of business processes.

The challenge becomes even more pronounced as we move toward autonomous systems. Consider a customer support professional who uses AI to summarize cases, retrieve knowledge articles, and prepare responses. These capabilities make the individual more productive. But imagine a future where AI agents can resolve a substantial portion of routine support requests independently, including gathering diagnostic information, executing approved corrective actions, and confirming resolution.

The employee's role may increasingly involve managing complex escalations, improving the knowledge that powers those agents, analyzing recurring customer problems, and identifying opportunities to improve the product itself. Simply knowing how to use the original AI assistant will not prepare that employee for this evolution. They will need to understand how intelligent workflows operate, how to evaluate their performance, how to recognize failures, and how to contribute to their continuous improvement.

This is why I believe organizations need to move beyond AI literacy toward professional development designed for an AI enabled workplace. AI literacy teaches people what the technology can do. Professional development prepares them to contribute meaningfully as the technology changes how work is performed.

The difference matters because adoption alone does not guarantee relevance. The objective should not be to create employees who are simply good at using AI. It should be to develop professionals whose expertise becomes more valuable because they understand how to work with increasingly intelligent systems.

## The Leadership Responsibility We Cannot Delegate

There is a tendency in technology transformations to place much of the responsibility for adaptation on employees. We encourage people to take ownership of their careers, remain curious, develop new skills, and embrace change. These expectations are reasonable. Individuals have an important responsibility to invest in their own development.

But there is a problem when organizations treat adaptation entirely as an individual obligation. Employees cannot prepare effectively for a future they do not understand, particularly when leaders themselves have not articulated how technology will change the organization's operating model.

Imagine telling a team that AI will transform their function, encouraging everyone to complete training, and then providing no clarity about how responsibilities are expected to evolve. Employees may become proficient with the technology while remaining uncertain about what that proficiency means for their careers. Some will experiment enthusiastically. Others will hesitate, concerned that demonstrating how easily their work can be automated may undermine the value of their positions.

That hesitation should not automatically be interpreted as resistance to change. It may be a rational response to uncertainty.

This is where leadership becomes particularly important. We need to create a clearer connection between AI adoption, the evolution of work, and professional development. If an organization expects AI to automate portions of a function, leaders should begin identifying the capabilities that will become more important as a result.

If technical execution becomes increasingly automated, perhaps system design, architecture, governance, and customer problem solving become areas of greater emphasis. If routine support activities become more autonomous, perhaps the organization needs stronger capabilities in exception management, knowledge engineering, and customer experience improvement.

These transitions require deliberate planning. Training programs must connect to future responsibilities. Employees need opportunities to apply new capabilities. Performance expectations must evolve. Career pathways should reflect emerging roles rather than relying exclusively on traditional job descriptions.

And where automation may genuinely reduce the number of positions required, leaders need to communicate with transparency and respect. We cannot promise that every employee will retain the same role. We can, however, make thoughtful decisions about workforce transitions, provide reasonable opportunities for development, and avoid allowing uncertainty to become a substitute for communication.

**Preparing people for the future is a shared responsibility. Employees must be willing to grow, and leaders must create the conditions that make growth possible.**

## The Three Conversations Leaders Should Be Having

As I think about how leaders should communicate AI's impact on work, I believe three messages are particularly important.

The first is that **your role may change, but your contribution can grow**. This acknowledges that technological progress will reshape responsibilities without assuming that every change represents a loss of professional value. Employees need to understand which activities may become automated and where their expertise can create greater impact. The conversation should focus on the future contribution of the individual, not simply the preservation of their existing tasks.

The second is that **your experience matters, but your ability to adapt will matter more**. This recognizes the value of accumulated knowledge while challenging the assumption that past success guarantees future relevance. Experienced professionals have an opportunity to combine their understanding of customers, systems, and business processes with emerging AI capabilities. Their advantage will increasingly depend on how effectively they translate that experience into new ways of working.

The third is that **preparing for the future is a shared responsibility**. Employees should take ownership of learning, experimentation, and professional growth. Leaders must provide direction, development opportunities, and an environment where people can apply what they learn. These messages are more demanding than simply reassuring employees that AI will not replace them. They require leaders to acknowledge uncertainty, make decisions about the future of work, and invest in the people who will help build it.

But they also establish a more credible foundation for trust. And trust matters because meaningful transformation depends on people being willing to participate in changing the very processes and responsibilities that define their work today.

## The Future of Work Is a Leadership Decision

Technological progress will influence how organizations operate, but technology alone does not determine how people experience that change. Leaders make decisions about where AI is deployed, which processes are redesigned, how productivity gains are reinvested, and what opportunities are created for employees.

An organization can use AI primarily to reduce operating costs. It can also use AI to expand capacity, improve customer experiences, create new services, and enable employees to take on more valuable responsibilities. In practice, many organizations will pursue a combination of these objectives.

The important point is that the consequences for employees are shaped not only by what AI can do, but also by the strategic choices leaders make.

This is particularly relevant as intelligent agents begin assuming greater responsibility for execution. When technology can perform more of the operational work, leaders must reconsider how human contribution is defined, developed, and rewarded. We may need fewer people manually coordinating routine activities, but greater expertise in designing workflows, validating outcomes, managing risk, and solving problems that require contextual judgment.

Some roles will expand. Others will change significantly. Some may disappear. The challenge is ensuring that workforce planning evolves alongside technology strategy rather than becoming an afterthought.

For leaders, this means moving beyond conversations about AI adoption and beginning to ask more fundamental questions. What will our teams be responsible for in three years? Which capabilities will become less important? Which will become essential? How are we preparing our employees for those changes today? And perhaps most importantly, are we creating real opportunities for people to grow into the future we are asking them to embrace?

These questions cannot be answered by purchasing another AI platform or launching another training course. They require leadership judgment, organizational commitment, and a willingness to rethink how we develop talent.

## Stop Promising Stability. Start Building Readiness.

Throughout history, technological progress has changed the relationship between people and work. Entire industries have emerged, professions have evolved, and capabilities once considered essential have gradually become less relevant.

Artificial intelligence is accelerating this process because it is beginning to affect not only physical and repetitive work, but also activities involving knowledge, analysis, communication, and decision making. That makes this transition particularly personal for many professionals. People who have spent years developing expertise are now being asked to reconsider how that expertise will remain valuable.

The response from leadership cannot simply be reassurance, nor should it be fear. We need a more thoughtful conversation that recognizes the uncertainty of technological change while giving people a meaningful opportunity to prepare.

We should be honest that some jobs will change and others may disappear. We should recognize that learning new tools is necessary but not sufficient. And we should accept that helping employees remain relevant requires more than encouraging them to take responsibility for their own development.

It requires organizations to invest in the future capabilities of their people with the same seriousness that they invest in the technology itself.

I believe the most important promise a leader can make is not that someone's job will remain unchanged. It is that we will be transparent about where the organization is heading, create opportunities for people to develop, and help them prepare for the responsibilities that come next.

That promise does not eliminate uncertainty. It does not guarantee employment or make difficult workforce decisions disappear. But it creates something more meaningful than reassurance. It creates a foundation for adaptability, trust, and shared progress.

**The future of work will not be defined only by what artificial intelligence becomes capable of doing. It will also be defined by how thoughtfully we prepare people for the work that remains, the work that changes, and the work that has yet to be created.**

And that may be one of the most important leadership responsibilities of the AI era.
