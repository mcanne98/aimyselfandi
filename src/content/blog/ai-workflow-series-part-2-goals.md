Last week, I introduced an idea that has fundamentally changed the way I work with artificial intelligence. It wasn't a new model, a new feature, or a clever prompt. It was the realization that the biggest gains I've experienced with AI haven't come from asking better questions. They've come from thinking more clearly before I ask anything at all.

That realization became the foundation of what I now call The AI Workflow Framework. Rather than treating AI as a tool that simply responds to prompts, I began thinking of it as a collaborator that performs best when it understands not only the task, but the outcome I'm trying to achieve. Like any good collaborator, it needs direction before it needs instructions.

Throughout this series, we'll walk through each stage of that framework.

```
/goals
      ↓
/context
      ↓
/assumptions
      ↓
/plan
      ↓
/draft
      ↓
/review
      ↓
/iterate
      ↓
/action
```

Today, I'd like to start at the very beginning. Not with prompts. Not with context. Not even with planning. I'd like to start with goals.

At first glance, this may seem almost too obvious to deserve its own article. After all, everyone knows goals are important. We've spent decades learning about SMART goals, OKRs, KPIs, and strategic planning. Surely we already understand the importance of defining success before beginning the work.

And yet, the moment we open an AI chat window, many of us forget that discipline entirely.

We immediately begin describing the task. "Write me a presentation." "Summarize this document." "Create a project plan." "Draft an email." None of those are goals. They're requests for work.

There's an important difference between telling AI what to do and explaining what success actually looks like. One defines the activity. The other defines the outcome. Experienced leaders have always understood that those are not the same thing, yet when we work with AI, it's surprisingly easy to blur the distinction.

The more I've reflected on thousands of conversations I've had with AI over the past few years, the more convinced I've become that this is where most conversations begin to drift. We assume the model will infer what we're trying to accomplish. Sometimes it does. Often it gets close enough that we don't notice what's missing until much later in the conversation.

The challenge isn't that AI lacks intelligence. The challenge is that it lacks intent.

It has no way of knowing whether you're writing a presentation to secure funding, persuade a board, educate a customer, or simply organize your own thinking. From its perspective, all of those requests look remarkably similar. Yet from your perspective, they require entirely different outputs.

That distinction became clear to me while preparing an executive presentation earlier this year.

The presentation itself wasn't particularly unusual. It focused on AI strategy, something I've spent countless hours thinking about and discussing with executive teams. I had all the information I needed. Research wasn't the problem. The challenge was deciding exactly what I wanted the presentation to accomplish.

Was I trying to educate the audience about emerging technology? Was I trying to persuade them to invest? Was I trying to help them think differently about organizational change? Or was I trying to provide a practical framework they could immediately apply within their own teams?

Those questions mattered far more than the slides themselves.

Had I simply asked AI to create a presentation on AI strategy, it would almost certainly have produced something competent. The slides would have been structured. The arguments would have been logical. The visuals might even have been compelling.

But competence wasn't the objective. Transformation was.

I didn't want executives to leave saying, "That was an interesting presentation." I wanted them to leave questioning how they approached AI inside their organizations.

Those are two completely different goals.

The slides might look similar. The outcome would not.

That experience forced me to rethink something I had taken for granted. I realized I had been spending far too much time refining prompts and far too little time defining success. The prompt wasn't the problem. The absence of a clearly articulated outcome was.

Since then, I've noticed the same pattern appearing almost everywhere.

When someone tells me that AI didn't produce what they were hoping for, my first question is rarely, "What prompt did you use?"

Instead, I ask something much simpler.

"What were you trying to accomplish?"

Surprisingly often, the answer isn't clear.

"I wanted it to help me write."

"I needed a report."

"I wanted a project plan."

Those responses sound reasonable until you examine them more closely.

Writing isn't the objective. A report isn't the objective. A project plan isn't the objective. They're all artifacts. They're outputs. They're things we produce along the way to achieving something else.

The real objective usually lives one level higher.

Perhaps the report is meant to secure additional funding. Perhaps the project plan is designed to align stakeholders before kickoff. Perhaps the email is intended to rebuild trust with an important customer. Perhaps the presentation is meant to change how an executive team thinks about AI.

Those are goals.

The document is simply the vehicle.

Once you begin looking at AI conversations through that lens, you start noticing something fascinating. AI rarely struggles with producing documents. It struggles because it has never been told why those documents matter.

That's when I began using a term that has become a regular part of my own thinking.

**Goal Drift.**

Goal Drift describes the gradual movement away from the original business outcome during an AI conversation. It rarely happens all at once. Instead, it's subtle.

You begin with an important objective. A few prompts later, you're debating the wording of a heading. Then you're refining formatting. Then you're asking for better transitions. Then you're reorganizing paragraphs. Thirty minutes later, you've produced a beautifully written document.

But you've completely forgotten why you were writing it.

AI has become exceptionally good at helping you perfect something that may no longer serve the outcome you originally cared about.

And the dangerous part is that it doesn't feel like failure.

It feels like progress.
