AI is becoming extraordinarily good at producing work that looks finished. A strategy can be structured beautifully. A presentation can tell a compelling story. A technical recommendation can sound authoritative. A customer communication can be polished enough to send almost immediately. The quality of the language has improved so dramatically that we're beginning to encounter a very different problem.

Something can sound right without actually being right.

That distinction becomes increasingly important as AI takes on more of the production work. When creating a polished document required hours of human effort, much of the review naturally happened during creation. We questioned ideas while writing them, reconsidered arguments while organizing them, and often discovered gaps simply because producing the work forced us to think through it.

AI compresses that process. A document that once took hours can now appear in seconds, which means some of the thinking that naturally occurred during creation can disappear with it. The responsibility doesn't go away. It simply moves somewhere else.

It moves to review.

And that is where we are in The AI Workflow Framework.

The complete workflow is beginning to take shape:

```
Goals → Context → Assumptions → Plan → Draft → Review → Iterate → Action
```

We are no longer trying to get AI to produce something.

We have something.

Now we need to determine whether it is actually good.

That requires a very different skill.

## The Review Has to Change

For years, reviewing a document largely meant editing it. We corrected grammar, changed words, reorganized paragraphs, adjusted formatting, and improved readability. We asked whether something sounded professional, whether the argument flowed, or whether the message could be clearer.

Those things still matter.

But AI can now do most of them remarkably well.

Ask Claude to improve the grammar and it will. Ask it to make something more concise and it will. Ask it to restructure an argument, change the tone, improve readability, or rewrite something for an executive audience and it can usually do it in seconds.

That means spending most of our review time editing sentences is increasingly a poor use of human judgment.

Human review needs to move up a level.

Instead of asking whether the sentence is good, ask whether the argument is good. Instead of asking whether the presentation looks convincing, ask whether the recommendation deserves our confidence. Instead of asking whether the document flows, ask whether the decision it supports is actually the right one.

The question is no longer simply "Is this well written?"

The more important question is "Is this right?"

That sounds like a small distinction.

It isn't.

It represents a fundamental change in where human value sits in an AI-enabled workflow.

## Polished Does Not Mean Proven

One of AI's greatest strengths is also one of the reasons review matters so much.

AI is exceptionally good at confidence.

A weak recommendation can be expressed beautifully. An incomplete analysis can be organized logically. An assumption can be presented like a fact. A conclusion based on insufficient evidence can still sound completely reasonable.

The quality of the presentation can easily exceed the quality of the underlying thinking.

This creates what I think of as the **Confidence Gap**.

The Confidence Gap is the distance between how convincing an AI-generated answer sounds and how confident we should actually be in it.

As AI improves, that gap becomes harder to see.

Poorly written work naturally creates friction. We slow down. We question it. We notice inconsistencies. But polished work lowers our defenses because it feels complete. The language flows. The headings make sense. The recommendations appear thoughtful.

That feeling of completeness can become dangerous if we allow presentation quality to become a substitute for judgment.

The objective of review is therefore not to make AI's work sound more confident.

It is to determine how much confidence the work actually deserves.

## Review the Thinking Behind the Work

This changes what a good review looks like.

The first question should be about alignment. Does the output actually solve the problem we originally defined? It is surprisingly easy for an AI conversation to drift. A document can become increasingly sophisticated while slowly moving away from the original objective.

Then comes evidence. Which conclusions are supported by facts, and which are interpretations? Where did the information come from? What would need to be validated before acting on the recommendation?

Then risk. What could make this recommendation wrong? What happens if one of the underlying assumptions changes? What second-order consequences haven't been considered?

Then blind spots. Whose perspective is missing? What would someone who disagrees with the recommendation say? What information could materially change the conclusion?

And finally, decision quality. After reading this, is it clear what should happen next? Does the recommendation create enough confidence to act, or has the document simply organized information without helping anyone make a decision?

These are very different questions from asking AI to "make this better."

They are also much more valuable.

## Claude Should Challenge the Draft, Not Approve It

There is another important change that happens during review.

AI should stop being the author and start becoming the challenger.

If Claude helped create the first draft, asking it simply to review its own work can reinforce the same reasoning that produced the document in the first place. A better approach is to deliberately change its role.

Ask Claude to find the weakest argument.

Ask it which claims require stronger evidence.

Ask it to identify where assumptions are being presented as facts.

Ask what a skeptical executive, customer, architect, clinician, or regulator might challenge.

Ask what would need to be true for the recommendation to fail.

Ask it to argue against the conclusion.

This creates something extremely valuable in knowledge work: productive disagreement.

The objective isn't to make Claude negative. It is to introduce friction into a process that has become almost frictionless.

Because friction, when applied deliberately, improves judgment.

## This Matters Even More in Healthcare

The distinction between editing and reviewing becomes particularly important when AI touches healthcare.

Imagine an AI-generated recommendation for changing an implementation approach for a clinical workflow. The document might be excellent. The reasoning may appear logical. The recommendation could promise improved efficiency and reduced administrative effort.

An editorial review might improve the wording.

An executive review asks different questions.

Does this reflect how clinicians actually work? Could the change create additional documentation burden? Are there downstream implications for integrations, reporting, medication workflows, or clinical documentation? What assumptions were made about customer readiness? What happens during a shift change? What happens when the workflow encounters an exception rather than the ideal scenario?

Suddenly, the review is no longer about the document.

It's about the consequences of the document.

That distinction matters enormously.

And while healthcare makes the stakes particularly visible, the principle applies everywhere. A software architecture decision creates consequences. A pricing recommendation creates consequences. A customer communication creates consequences. A hiring strategy creates consequences.

The more AI participates in creating recommendations, the more important it becomes for humans to evaluate their consequences.

## Human Value Is Moving Toward Judgment

There is a larger shift happening underneath all of this.

As AI becomes better at production, the value of production itself begins to decline.

Writing a professional document used to demonstrate expertise. Creating a detailed analysis required significant effort. Producing a sophisticated presentation signaled capability.

AI is rapidly democratizing all of those activities.

Almost anyone can now create something that looks impressive.

That doesn't make expertise less valuable.

It changes where expertise becomes visible.

Expertise increasingly shows up in the ability to recognize what is missing. To challenge a recommendation that sounds convincing. To understand consequences that aren't obvious. To distinguish correlation from causation. To know when more evidence is required. To recognize when the technically correct answer is operationally unrealistic.

In other words, expertise moves from production to judgment.

That may become one of the most important professional skills in an AI-enabled workplace.

The future knowledge worker isn't simply someone who can create.

It's someone who can evaluate what has been created.

## Review Like an Executive

Reviewing like an executive doesn't require an executive title.

It describes a way of thinking.

An editor asks whether the words work.

An executive asks whether the recommendation works.

An editor looks for mistakes.

An executive looks for consequences.

An editor improves the document.

An executive improves the decision the document is meant to support.

This is the mindset AI requires from all of us.

As machines become increasingly capable of producing polished work, our responsibility isn't to compete with them sentence by sentence. It is to provide the judgment, experience, skepticism, and accountability that determine whether the work should influence what happens next.

That is why review sits exactly where it does in The AI Workflow Framework.

Goals defined success.

Context created understanding.

Assumptions exposed uncertainty.

Planning organized the thinking.

Drafting made that thinking visible.

Review now challenges whether that thinking deserves our confidence.

And only after that should we improve it.
