*Why reducing concern about AI requires leaders to make the future more tangible for the people being asked to build it.*

In my [last article](https://blog.cedricanne.com/blog/stop-telling-employees-ai-wont-replace-their-jobs/), I argued that leaders need to move beyond reassuring employees that AI will not replace their jobs. As artificial intelligence assumes greater responsibility for execution, learning to use the technology will not necessarily protect someone from changes to their role or from decisions about how many people an organization needs. I suggested that career adaptability offers a more useful foundation for preparing people for this transition, and that building it is a shared responsibility between employees and leaders. But there is another dimension to this conversation that deserves closer attention. We are asking employees to help improve the very processes that justify their positions. Before expecting them to participate enthusiastically, we need to consider what that request looks like from their perspective.

Imagine an experienced employee who discovers that AI can perform a significant portion of a process they manage every week. They understand the work well enough to identify where the technology is useful, correct its mistakes, and develop an approach that produces reliable results. From the organization's perspective, this is precisely the behaviour we want to encourage. The employee is applying experience, demonstrating initiative, and helping the business improve. From the employee's perspective, however, the implications may be less straightforward. If I demonstrate that this work requires substantially less effort, what happens next? Will I be given more meaningful responsibilities? Will I simply be expected to produce more? Or will the organization conclude that it no longer needs as many people doing this work?

These are reasonable questions. An employee can recognize the value of AI, understand its potential, and still be uncertain about whether helping the organization adopt it will improve their own future. That uncertainty should influence how we interpret hesitation. Someone who is reluctant to share an automation opportunity may be making a careful assessment of the consequences. If we want people to contribute their expertise to changing how work is performed, we need to give them a credible reason to believe that their contribution will be considered when decisions are made about what follows.

I believe this creates an important leadership challenge. The people who understand a process most deeply are often the people best positioned to help redesign it. They know which exceptions matter, where information is unreliable, and why an apparently unnecessary step exists. Yet those same people may feel particularly exposed when the organization begins discussing automation. Without their involvement, we risk overlooking the knowledge required to make the change effective. Without clarity about their future, we risk asking them to participate in something they believe could work against their interests.

Reducing this concern requires practical decisions that employees can see and assess. There are three things I believe leaders can begin doing now.

The first is to make the anticipated changes specific enough for employees to understand and influence. Telling a team that AI will transform its function creates an expectation of change without providing much direction. People are left to interpret what that means for their responsibilities, their performance, and their future. A more useful starting point is to examine a particular workflow with the people who perform it. Which activities could be automated? Which decisions will continue to require human review? Where might the technology introduce additional work or new risks? What is the organization actually trying to achieve?

Consider a technical implementation team exploring AI to support configuration and documentation. The objective might be to reduce implementation delays, improve consistency, or increase the number of customers the team can support. Each objective has different implications for how work should be redesigned and how employees should prepare. Leaders should explain the intended outcome, acknowledge what remains uncertain, and make clear which decisions employees can still influence. Where reductions in staffing are being considered, that possibility should be addressed honestly. A practical starting point this week would be to bring one team together around one process and document the expected changes, the unanswered questions, and a date to revisit them. This gives employees a clearer basis for preparation and a meaningful opportunity to contribute.

The second is to explain how productivity gains are expected to be used before asking employees to deliver them. When AI reduces the effort required to complete an activity, the organization gains choices. It may use that capacity to address a backlog, improve service, pursue additional business, develop employees, or reduce costs. Those choices directly affect how people experience the change. If employees are asked to identify efficiencies without understanding what will happen when they succeed, concern about the consequences is understandable.

Leaders should establish a clear intention for how the benefits of an AI initiative will be applied. During an initial pilot, for example, a team might agree to use verified time savings to reduce overdue work and give employees supervised experience in responsibilities that are becoming more important. That commitment should be specific enough to review and realistic enough to honour. It should also account for the time required to check AI outputs and correct mistakes, because completing an initial task faster does not necessarily mean the entire process requires less effort. The objective is to create a visible connection between improving the work and creating opportunities for the people involved.

This does not mean leaders can guarantee that productivity improvements will never affect staffing. Commitments need to reflect the authority leaders actually have and the conditions the business faces. But we can explain what we intend to do, define the period over which that commitment applies, and communicate when circumstances change. A useful action this week would be to review an existing AI initiative and ask what its success would mean for the employees contributing to it. If the benefit to the organization is clear but the implications for employees remain undefined, that is a leadership question we need to resolve.

The third is to give employees practical experience in future responsibilities while they are still performing their current roles. In discussions about AI, we frequently suggest that people will spend less time on routine execution and more time applying judgment, managing exceptions, and improving outcomes. That transition requires deliberate development. An employee who has spent years handling individual support requests does not automatically become prepared to evaluate the performance of an automated support process. They may have valuable knowledge of customer problems, but they will need opportunities to apply that knowledge differently.

For example, a support professional could begin reviewing a small set of AI-assisted resolutions with an experienced colleague. They could assess whether the proposed actions were appropriate, identify missing context, and examine recurring problems that should be addressed in the underlying process. Over time, this provides experience in evaluating and improving automated work. It also gives the employee tangible evidence of how their expertise can remain useful as responsibilities evolve. A training course may help establish the foundation, but practical assignments, feedback, and protected time are what allow new capabilities to develop.

We should also consider how employees acquire the judgment we expect them to exercise. Many professionals develop expertise by working through routine activities, encountering exceptions, and learning from their mistakes. If we automate substantial portions of that work, we need to preserve opportunities for people to build the understanding that more advanced responsibilities require. Otherwise, we may remove part of the learning process while continuing to expect the same level of professional judgment. A practical step this week is to identify one emerging responsibility for someone affected by an AI initiative and create a supervised opportunity to practise it. That opportunity should connect to work the organization genuinely expects to need, with existing expectations adjusted to make room for learning.

These three actions will not remove every concern about AI and employment. Some roles may require fewer people, some transitions will be difficult, and development cannot guarantee that an appropriate position will be available for every individual. We should acknowledge those limitations. Employees deserve an honest account of what an organization can offer and what remains uncertain. At the same time, uncertainty does not prevent leaders from providing direction, involving people in decisions, and creating meaningful opportunities to prepare.

In my previous article, I described career adaptability as a shared responsibility. Making that responsibility real requires us to examine what happens when employees do everything we ask of them. When they learn the technology, contribute their expertise, and help improve productivity, how do we support their next contribution? The answer will influence whether employees experience AI adoption as an opportunity they can help shape or a change whose consequences they must simply accept.

I believe one of the most credible ways to reduce concern is to ensure that people can see a connection between the future we are building for the organization and the future they are preparing for themselves. Before asking employees to help AI perform more of their work, we should be prepared to explain how we will help them develop toward the responsibilities that follow.
